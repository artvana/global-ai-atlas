# Maryland SB 936 — Consumer Protection: High-Risk Artificial Intelligence Developer and Deployer Requirements

**Senate Bill No. 936 / House Bill No. 1331**

*Maryland General Assembly — 2025 Regular Session (Failed — Died in Committee)*

---

## Legislative Status

SB 936 and its House companion HB 1331 were introduced in the Maryland General Assembly's 2025 regular session. SB 936 had a hearing before the Senate Finance Committee on February 27, 2025, but did not advance to a floor vote. HB 1331 similarly failed to cross the legislative deadline. Both bills died in committee at the close of the 2025 session (Sine Die, April 2025). Neither bill was signed or vetoed.

**Note:** These bills are sometimes referenced in secondary sources as "SB 1050 / HB 1457," which do not correspond to AI-related legislation in the 2025 Maryland session. The accurate bill numbers for Maryland's 2025 comprehensive high-risk AI consumer protection legislation are SB 936 and HB 1331.

---

## Purpose and Scope

SB 936 would have established a comprehensive consumer protection framework for high-risk artificial intelligence systems in Maryland. The bill regulated two categories of actors:

- **Developers**: Entities that design, code, or produce high-risk AI systems for deployment or sale
- **Deployers**: Entities that use a high-risk AI system in commerce to make or inform consequential decisions about consumers

The bill defined **high-risk AI** by reference to use in consequential decision domains: employment, housing, credit and financial services, healthcare, education, and access to public accommodations.

---

## Key Provisions

### Developer Obligations

Beginning February 1, 2026 (as proposed), developers of high-risk AI systems would have been required to:

1. Use reasonable care to prevent known and reasonably foreseeable risks of **algorithmic discrimination** — defined as unlawful differential treatment or impact on consumers based on protected characteristics (age, color, disability, familial status, gender identity, genetic information, national origin, race, religion, sex, sexual orientation, or veteran status)
2. Provide deployers with detailed technical documentation covering:
   - The system's intended uses and known limitations
   - The nature and degree of consequential decisions it supports
   - Foreseeable risks of algorithmic discrimination
   - Data governance practices
   - Performance metrics and evaluation results
3. Make available a plain-language summary of the system for consumer-facing disclosure

### Deployer Obligations

Deployers would have been required to:

1. Implement a written **risk management policy** covering the organization's use of high-risk AI, updated at least annually
2. Complete a pre-deployment **impact assessment** before using any high-risk AI system, documenting:
   - The intended purpose and expected benefits of the system
   - Known and reasonably foreseeable risks of algorithmic discrimination
   - The quality, relevance, and limitations of data used by the system
   - Measures to mitigate identified risks
3. **Notify consumers** at or prior to the point of a consequential decision that a high-risk AI system was used or consulted in making that decision

### Consumer Rights

Consumers subject to high-risk AI decisions would have had:

- The right to **correct inaccurate personal data** used by the system
- The right to **appeal adverse decisions** informed by high-risk AI
- The right to receive a plain-language explanation of the decision

### Safe Harbor

Deployers that substantially complied with a recognized AI risk management framework (such as the NIST AI RMF) would have qualified for a safe harbor from liability under the Act.

### Enforcement

The **Maryland Attorney General** would have had exclusive authority to enforce the Act. The bill did not create a private right of action for individual consumers.

---

## Opposition and Failure

Industry groups, including the Maryland Tech Council, opposed the bill on grounds that mandatory impact assessments and documentation requirements would impose excessive compliance costs on software developers and create legal uncertainty. The bills failed to advance out of committee, continuing a national pattern in 2025 where comprehensive state AI bills — including similar measures in Georgia, Hawaii, New Mexico, and Vermont — failed to become law.

---

## Context

SB 936 was part of a wave of state-level comprehensive AI bills modeled substantially on **Colorado SB 205 (2024)**, the first state law in the U.S. to impose comprehensive developer and deployer obligations for high-risk AI. Maryland's failure to pass the bill leaves the state without a comprehensive AI consumer protection statute, though Maryland did enact narrower AI legislation in 2025, including HB 820 (AI in health insurance utilization review) and HB 956 (AI Working Group).
