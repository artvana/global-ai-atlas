# Online Safety (Relief and Accountability) Act 2025

**(No. 23 of 2025)**

*Passed by Parliament: 5 November 2025*
*Assented to by the President: 25 November 2025*

---

## PART 1 — PRELIMINARY

### Section 1 — Short title and commencement

The Act is the Online Safety (Relief and Accountability) Act 2025 and comes into operation on a date the Minister appoints by Gazette notification.

### Section 2 — Purposes of Act

- **(a)** to provide persons affected by online harmful activity with timely means of redress and statutory rights of action and remedies in court proceedings;
- **(b)** to improve and promote online safety for persons;
- **(c)** to deter and prevent online harmful activity and mitigate harm caused to persons by such activity;
- **(d)** to promote accountability and responsible and reasonable conduct among participants and stakeholders in the online environment.

### Section 3 — General interpretation

Key definitions:

- **"administrator"** of an online location — a person who develops/maintains it, organises/manages/supervises its use, manages access or membership, or has authority over what may be included or excluded (editorial control). Excludes online service providers, internet access providers, and app distribution providers acting solely in that capacity; excludes public agencies.
- **"communicate"/"communication"** — making material available to one or more persons in Singapore on or through the Internet. Administrators, online service providers, internet access providers, and app distribution providers do not "communicate" material communicated by another person merely because they provide the platform.
- **"inauthentic material"** — audio, visual or audiovisual material that (a) has been altered or generated using digital means (including generative AI), (b) is a false or misleading depiction of the victim's words, actions or conduct, and (c) is realistic enough that a reasonable person would believe the victim said or did the things depicted.
- **"intimate image or recording"** — an image or recording (including altered or AI-generated) of a person's genital/anal region, female breasts, or of the person doing a private act.
- **"online harmful activity"** — any of the 13 categories defined in Part 3, plus any prescribed type.
- **"online location"** — any internet domain, website, webpage, chatroom, chat group, channel, group, forum, social media account page, or other location accessible by the Internet.
- **"online service provider"** — provider (other than a public agency) of an online service that is not an internet access service or app distribution service.
- **"prescribed online service provider"** — an online service provider or class prescribed by regulations.
- **"publish"/"publication"** — making material available on or through the Internet such that it is heard, seen or otherwise perceived by the public in Singapore or a section of the public.
- **"Singapore account"** — an online account whose owner is a Singapore citizen, permanent resident, Singapore-registered entity, or individual physically present in Singapore.

### Section 4 — Application of Act

Public agencies are exempt from receiving Part 5 directions or orders, may not commence civil claims under Parts 10–12, and are not liable under those Parts.

---

## PART 2 — ADMINISTRATION OF ACT

### Section 5 — Commissioner of Online Safety

The Minister appoints a Commissioner of Online Safety to administer Parts 2–9, provide timely redress to persons affected by online harmful activity, and promote online safety. Deputy and Assistant Commissioners may also be appointed. All are deemed public servants.

### Section 6 — Authorised officers

The Commissioner may appoint authorised officers and delegate functions to them, subject to conditions.

### Section 8 — Advisory guidelines

The Commissioner may issue, amend or revoke advisory guidelines on the Act's operation and on conduct that promotes online safety. Advisory guidelines have no legislative effect.

## PART 3 — ONLINE HARMFUL ACTIVITIES

Defines 13 statutory categories of online harmful activity:

### Section 9 — Online harassment

Communication of online material that a reasonable person would conclude is (a) threatening, abusive, insulting, sexual or indecent **and** (b) likely to cause a person harassment, alarm, distress or humiliation. Excludes sexual/indecent material with a legitimate science, medicine, education or art purpose. A third party may be a victim if the communication is likely to cause them harm by reason of their personal relationship with the addressee. Multiple communications may be aggregated.

### Section 10 — Doxxing

Publication of **identity information** of a person (including name, address, email, phone, date of birth, NRIC/passport number, photographs/videos, or family/employment/education information) which a reasonable person would conclude was likely intended to cause the victim harassment, alarm, distress or humiliation.

### Section 11 — Non-consensual disclosure of private information

Publication of private information about a person without consent where a reasonable person would conclude it is likely to cause harassment, alarm, distress or humiliation. "Private information" means information not previously widely available to the public.

### Section 12 — Online stalking

A course of online conduct (on 1 protracted occasion, or 2+ occasions) by a stalker in respect of a victim, involving acts associated with stalking (e.g. communications to/about the victim, presence at online locations the victim visits, making a presence felt), that a reasonable person would conclude is likely to cause harassment, alarm, distress or humiliation.

### Section 13 — Intimate image abuse

Communication of online material containing — without consent — an intimate image or recording of the victim, an offer to sell/distribute one, or an advertisement of one, that a reasonable person would conclude is likely to cause harassment, alarm, distress or humiliation. Expressly includes digitally-altered and AI-generated material.

### Section 14 — Image-based child abuse

Communication of online material containing a child abuse image/recording (including AI-generated content) of a person below 16 years of age depicting sexual abuse, sexual pose/activity, or exposed genital/anal/breast region, or material designed to lead to such images (including hyperlinks, phrases, hints).

### Section 15 — Online impersonation

Online activity by X pretending to be the victim without consent in a manner that would lead a reasonable person to believe the activity was conducted by the victim. Excludes parody/satire that no reasonable person would believe is made by the person.

### Section 16 — Inauthentic material abuse

Communication of inauthentic material of a victim (audio, visual or audiovisual material altered or generated using digital means including generative AI, constituting a false/misleading depiction of the victim's words/actions realistic enough to deceive) that a reasonable person would conclude is likely to cause harassment, alarm, distress or humiliation.

### Section 17 — Publication of false material

Publication of online material containing a false statement of fact about the victim that a reasonable person would conclude is likely to cause harm to the victim.

### Section 18 — Publication of statement harmful to reputation

Publication of online material containing a statement (fact or opinion) likely to cause harm to the victim's reputation and any other additional harm.

### Section 19 — Online instigation of disproportionate harm

Communication of online material containing a statement that tends to instigate the public to act in response to alleged conduct of the victim, where the instigation would increase the risk of harm to the victim and the act/harm would be disproportionate to any wrongfulness of the victim's conduct. Carve-outs for electoral, political, and public interest communications.

### Section 20 — Incitement of enmity

Communication of online material that a reasonable person would conclude incites feelings of enmity, hatred or hostility against any group in Singapore. Excludes private/domestic communications. Relevant factors include dehumanisation, intensity of feelings incited, and tendency to incite criminal acts.

### Section 21 — Incitement of violence

Communication of online material that a reasonable person would conclude incites one or more persons to use unlawful force or violence against any group in Singapore.

## PART 4 — REPORTS

### Section 22 — Eligibility to make report

A victim of online harmful activity (excluding incitement offenses) who is a Singapore citizen, permanent resident, or person with a prescribed connection to Singapore may make a report to the Commissioner. Reports may be made on a victim's behalf by a parent/guardian (if under 18), or a person with the victim's written authorisation.

### Section 24 — Assessment and investigation

The Commissioner assesses reports and may seek information, investigate, or discontinue in specified circumstances.

### Section 25 — Trivial, frivolous or vexatious reports

The Commissioner may dismiss trivial, frivolous, vexatious or bad-faith reports. Persistent abusers may be barred from making reports for up to 3 years. This decision is final.

---

## PART 5 — POWERS TO GIVE DIRECTIONS AND ORDERS

### Section 26 — Part 5 directions — general

After receiving a report, the Commissioner may issue Part 5 directions where there is reason to suspect online harmful activity. Directions may be given to any individual or entity inside or outside Singapore and may require acts inside or outside Singapore. For publications of false material (s. 17) and statements harmful to reputation (s. 18), only right-of-reply directions may generally be given.

### Section 27 — Relevant factors

Degree of harm; number of persons harmed; manner and circumstances; reasonableness of the conduct; likelihood of further activity; public interest.

### Section 28 — Recipients

Depending on the direction type, recipients may be: the communicator of relevant material; an administrator of the relevant online location; an online service provider; or a prescribed online service provider.

### Section 29 — Stop communication direction

Requires the recipient to stop communicating the relevant material in Singapore.

### Section 30 — Stop communication (class of material) direction

Requires the recipient to stop communicating online material in a relevant class.

### Section 31 — Restraining direction

Requires the recipient to refrain from continuing or repeating the relevant online activity in respect of the victim.

### Section 32 — Access disabling direction

Requires an online service provider to disable end-user access in Singapore to the relevant material.

### Section 33 — Access disabling (class of material) direction

Requires a prescribed online service provider to disable end-user access in Singapore to any material in the relevant class.

### Section 34 — Right-of-reply (user) direction

Requires the communicator or administrator to communicate a reply notice.

### Section 35 — Right-of-reply (online service) direction

Requires an online service provider to communicate a reply notice on its service.

### Section 37 — Labelling direction

Requires the communicator or administrator to label the relevant material with a specified label.

### Section 38 — Account restriction (administrator) direction

Requires an administrator to restrict a relevant Singapore account from accessing or interacting at the online location for a specified period.

### Section 39 — Account restriction (online service) direction

Requires an online service provider to restrict a Singapore account from accessing or using its service or specified functionality for a specified period.

### Section 40 — Engagement reduction direction

Requires a prescribed online service provider to reduce the engagement or amplification of the relevant material on its service (e.g., suppressing recommendations or distribution).

### Section 41 — Supplementary provisions

No general right to be heard before a direction is given. Directions must identify the relevant material/location/activity sufficiently for compliance and state the recipient's rights of reconsideration and appeal.

### Section 43 — Order following non-compliance

Where a recipient fails to comply with a Part 5 direction, the Commissioner may issue an access blocking order or app removal order.

### Section 44 — Access blocking order

Requires an internet access service provider to disable access by end-users in Singapore to the relevant online location.

### Section 45 — App removal order

Requires an app distribution service provider to stop distributing the relevant app to persons in Singapore.

## PART 6 — INFORMATION AND END-USER IDENTITY MATTERS

### Section 48 — Retention of records

The Commissioner may require an online service provider to retain relevant records for one year or longer. Failure: fine up to **$5,000** (individual) or **$10,000** (other).

### Section 49 — Power to require information and documents

The Commissioner may require any person to provide information, documents, or access to computers/devices (including providing authentication credentials). Failure: fine up to **$5,000** (individual, plus up to 3 months imprisonment) or **$10,000** (other).

### Section 52 — End-user identity information collection notice

Where the Commissioner suspects an end-user engaged in online harmful activity via a prescribed online service provider, the Commissioner may require the provider to obtain specified identity information from the end-user as a condition of continued service and provide it to the Commissioner. Failure: individual — fine up to **$20,000** or 12 months imprisonment or both, plus **$2,000/day** continuing; other — fine up to **$500,000**, plus **$50,000/day** continuing.

### Section 53 — Disclosure of end-user identity information

The Commissioner may disclose end-user identity/contact information to eligible applicants for prescribed purposes, subject to conditions. Breaching conditions: fine up to **$20,000** or 12 months imprisonment (individual); **$50,000** (other).

### Section 54 — Preservation of secrecy

Specified persons must not disclose information acquired in their capacity except in performance of duties or as required by law. Breach: fine up to **$20,000** or 12 months imprisonment.

---

## PART 7 — RECONSIDERATION AND APPEALS

### Sections 56–59 — Reconsideration

Eligible persons (including the victim/report-maker, the direction recipient, and prescribed affected persons) may apply to the Commissioner to reconsider decisions, directions, or orders. The Commissioner may confirm, vary, substitute, or revoke the original decision.

### Sections 60–67 — Online Safety Appeal Panel and Appeal Committees

An **Online Safety Appeal Panel** is established, from which **Appeal Committees** hear appeals against reconsidered decisions. Appeal Committees may confirm, vary, substitute, or revoke directions or orders.

---

## PART 8 — OFFENCES AND ENFORCEMENT

### Section 69 — Offence of providing false information

Providing false or misleading information in connection with reports or applications under the Act, knowing or being reckless as to its falsity; or intentionally altering, suppressing, or destroying required documents. Penalty: fine up to **$20,000** or 12 months imprisonment (individual); **$50,000** (other).

### Section 70 — Publicity regarding non-compliance

The Commissioner may publish statements about non-compliance with directions on the official website. Published statements have absolute privilege.

### Section 71 — Offences of non-compliance with directions

Failure to comply with a Part 5 direction: individual — fine up to **$20,000** or 12 months imprisonment or both, plus **$2,000/day** continuing; other — fine up to **$500,000**, plus **$50,000/day** continuing.

### Section 72 — Offences of non-compliance with orders

- Access blocking order non-compliance: fine up to **$250,000**, plus **$20,000/day** continuing.
- App removal order non-compliance: fine up to **$500,000**, plus **$50,000/day** continuing.

### Section 73 — Defences

Duty of confidentiality or privacy is **not** a defence. It **is** a defence to show it was not reasonably practicable to do more than was done, or there was no better practicable means.

### Section 76 — Online harmful activity remedial initiative

The Public Prosecutor may consider remedial actions (relating to online safety, remedy of harm, rehabilitation, or community contribution) specified by the Commissioner when deciding whether to institute proceedings for non-compliance offences.

## PART 10 — ONLINE HARMFUL ACTIVITIES — TORTS

Creates statutory torts giving victims direct civil causes of action:

### Section 83 — Tort of intimate image abuse

A person must not communicate intimate image abuse. Defences: conduct was reasonable; reasonable grounds to believe the victim consented; did not know and had no reason to suspect the material was an intimate image or recording.

### Section 84 — Tort of image-based child abuse

Equivalent civil tort for image-based child abuse.

### Section 85 — Tort of online impersonation

Equivalent civil tort for online impersonation. Defence: conduct was reasonable.

### Section 86 — Tort of inauthentic material abuse

Equivalent civil tort for inauthentic material abuse (including AI-generated deepfakes). Defences: conduct was reasonable; did not know and had no reason to suspect the material was inauthentic.

### Section 87 — Tort of online instigation of disproportionate harm

Civil tort for online instigation of disproportionate harm. Defence: conduct was reasonable.

### Section 88 — Tort of incitement of violence

Civil tort for incitement of violence. Each member of the targeted group is a victim. Defence: conduct was reasonable.

---

## PART 11 — ADMINISTRATORS — DUTIES AND TORTS

Applies to: online harassment, doxxing, online stalking, intimate image abuse, image-based child abuse, online impersonation, inauthentic material abuse, and incitement of violence.

### Section 90 — Tort of facilitating or permitting applicable online harmful activity

A person must not develop, maintain or administer an online location in a manner that facilitates or permits any applicable online harmful activity, intending or knowing that such activity is likely to be conducted there. Defence: respondent's conduct was reasonable in the circumstances.

### Section 91 — Tort of failing to respond reasonably to online harm notice

A victim may send an administrator a written **online harm notice**. The administrator owes a duty to assess whether the activity occurred and, if so, take reasonable steps to address it within a reasonable time. Failure is presumed if the material remains accessible after a reasonable period following notice. Defence: notice was not received through no fault of the administrator's.

### Section 92 — Tort of sending frivolous or false online harm notices

Administrators have a right of action against persons who send online harm notices that are frivolous or false in a material way.

---

## PART 12 — ONLINE SERVICE PROVIDERS — DUTIES AND TORTS

### Section 94 — Tort of failing to respond reasonably to online harm notice

Mirrors Part 11 obligations for online service providers. For **prescribed online service providers**, a mandatory **prescribed period** replaces "reasonable time" and a provider that responds in accordance with prescribed requirements is deemed compliant.

### Section 95 — Tort of sending frivolous or false online harm notices

Equivalent to section 92.

---

## PART 13 — DAMAGES AND REMEDIES

### Section 96 — Damages

Courts may award damages they think just and equitable. May include loss of future earnings and an account of profits. Maximum and minimum damages may be specified by regulation.

### Section 97 — Damages for incitement of violence

Courts may award damages to group members collectively; damages may be applied to purposes fulfilling the group's objectives.

### Section 98 — Enhanced damages

Courts may award enhanced damages (additional to general/special damages, distinct from punitive/aggravated damages) where a victim made a reasonable written request to address the harmful activity and the respondent failed without reasonable excuse to do so within a reasonable time.

### Section 99 — Reliefs and remedies

Courts may order respondents to: remove specified material; stop storing/transmitting it; suspend or delete specified online locations; stop the relevant online activity; restrict specified Singapore accounts; disable end-user access; and prevent specified persons from creating accounts interacting with Singapore users.

### Section 100 — Orders against third parties

In proceedings involving communication of offending material, courts may order third parties to stop communicating the material, require administrators to stop it from being communicated, and require online service providers to disable access for Singapore end-users.

## PART 15 — AMENDMENTS TO OTHER ACTS

### Section 109 — Amendment of Protection from Harassment Act 2014 (POHA)

- New orders against **administrators**: protection orders, expedited protection orders, stop publication orders, correction orders, and interim orders.
- **Stop publication (administrator) order** (new s. 15CA): court may order an administrator to stop publication of specified false material.
- **Correction (administrator) order** (new s. 15CB): court may order an administrator to publish a correction notice stating the court has found the material to contain a false statement of fact.
- Expedited protection orders against administrators available on prima facie evidence of imminent harm.
- "Alarm or distress" broadened to "alarm, distress or humiliation" throughout POHA.
