# Proposed Rule: Conflicts of Interest Associated with the Use of Predictive Data Analytics by Broker-Dealers and Investment Advisers

*Securities and Exchange Commission*
*Release No. IA-6383 / 34-97990*
*File No. S7-12-23*
*Proposed: July 26, 2023*
*Comment Period Closed: October 10, 2023*

---

## Summary

The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC or Commission) is proposing new rules under the Investment Advisers Act of 1940 and the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 to address conflicts of interest associated with broker-dealers' and investment advisers' use of predictive data analytics (PDA), including artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning (ML), and related technologies, in investor interactions.

The proposed rules would require broker-dealers and investment advisers (collectively, "covered firms") that use covered technologies in investor interactions to:

1. Evaluate whether such use involves or results in a conflict of interest that places the firm's or an associated person's interest ahead of the investor's interest;

2. Eliminate or neutralize the effect of any such conflict of interest; and

3. Establish, maintain, and enforce written policies and procedures reasonably designed to achieve compliance with the foregoing requirements.

---

## I. Background and Rationale

### A. The Growth of Predictive Data Analytics in Financial Services

Broker-dealers and investment advisers increasingly use advanced analytical tools — including machine learning models, natural language processing, deep learning networks, and other AI technologies — in their interactions with retail investors. These technologies are used in a range of investor-facing applications, including:

- **Robo-advisers and automated portfolio management**: Algorithms that construct, rebalance, and manage investment portfolios based on investor characteristics and goals.
- **Personalized communication and targeting**: Systems that analyze investor data to determine which products, services, or communications to show individual investors, and when.
- **Recommendation systems**: Algorithms that present or highlight investment products, trading opportunities, or financial products to investors based on predicted behavior or preferences.
- **Behavioral nudges**: Techniques (including gamification, push notifications, and interface design) that use data about investor behavior to influence investment decisions.
- **Chatbots and virtual assistants**: AI-powered investor-facing tools that respond to investor inquiries, provide account information, and may provide investment guidance.
- **Risk assessment and profiling**: Systems that assess investor risk tolerance, financial sophistication, or other characteristics.
- **Predictive analytics for sales and marketing**: Models that predict which investors are most likely to respond to offers for particular products.

### B. Conflicts of Interest Created by Predictive Data Analytics

The Commission is concerned that PDA technologies may be designed or used in ways that optimize for the financial interests of the firm at the expense of investors. Unlike a human financial adviser whose conflicts may be disclosed and understood, an AI or algorithmic system may systematically optimize for outcomes that generate revenue, fees, or other benefits for the firm in ways that are:

- **Opaque**: The optimization criteria are not disclosed to investors and may not be transparent even to the firm's own staff.
- **Scalable**: Algorithmic conflicts of interest can be applied simultaneously to millions of investors.
- **Subtle**: AI systems may learn to exploit behavioral biases or to exploit information asymmetries between the firm and the investor in ways that are difficult for investors to detect.
- **Persistent**: Unlike a human adviser who may be counseled or corrected, an AI system may continue to act on conflicted objectives unless affirmatively retrained or reprogrammed.

The Commission is particularly concerned about the following categories of AI-related conflicts of interest:

**1. Optimization for Revenue or Engagement Metrics**: PDA systems that are designed to maximize transaction volume, product sales, account engagement, or other revenue-generating metrics may recommend investments or courses of action that are not in the investor's best interest.

**2. Differential Treatment by Investor Profitability**: PDA systems that identify high-value investor segments and provide better service, information, or recommendations to those segments than to lower-value investors may violate obligations to treat investors fairly.

**3. Product Recommendation Conflicts**: PDA systems that recommend proprietary products over equivalent third-party products, or that recommend higher-fee products over lower-fee alternatives, without adequate disclosure, present conflicts that may not be in investors' best interests.

**4. Behavioral Exploitation**: PDA systems that identify and systematically exploit behavioral biases in individual investors — for example, systems that know certain investors are prone to loss aversion and use that information to push ill-timed trading — may harm investors.

**5. Data Advantage Conflicts**: Firms that use information derived from their own investor base to structure products or trading strategies that extract value from those same investors face structural conflicts of interest.

### C. Limitations of Existing Regulations

Existing regulations — including the Investment Advisers Act's anti-fraud provisions, Regulation Best Interest for broker-dealers, and the Investment Adviser fiduciary standard — address conflicts of interest through disclosure and general conduct standards. The Commission has determined that these existing frameworks may be inadequate to address PDA-related conflicts for several reasons:

- Disclosure alone may not protect investors when conflicts are embedded in opaque algorithmic systems that investors cannot meaningfully evaluate;
- The existing best interest and fiduciary standards were designed primarily with human advisers in mind and may require additional guidance to apply clearly to AI-driven decisions;
- The scale and sophistication of AI-related conflicts may require affirmative conflict elimination or neutralization, rather than merely disclosure, to adequately protect investors.

## II. Proposed Rules: Summary

### A. Proposed Rule 15l-2 Under the Exchange Act (Broker-Dealers)

The Commission proposes new Rule 15l-2 under the Securities Exchange Act of 1934. The proposed rule would apply to registered broker-dealers that use a covered technology in investor interactions.

**Covered Technologies**: Under the proposed rule, "covered technology" means any analytical, technological, or computational function, algorithm, model, correlation matrix, or similar method or process that optimizes for, predicts, guides, forecasts, or directs investment-related behaviors or outcomes of investors.

This definition is intended to capture a broad range of analytical tools — including AI models, ML algorithms, and similar technologies — used in investor-facing contexts. The definition focuses on the purpose (optimizing, predicting, guiding, or directing investor behavior or outcomes) rather than the underlying technology, to ensure that the rule remains applicable as technology evolves.

**Investor Interactions**: The proposed rule applies to the use of covered technologies in "investor interactions," defined to include the provision of investment information, investment advice, investment recommendations, solicitations, or account services by a covered firm or associated person to an investor.

### B. Proposed Rule under the Investment Advisers Act (Investment Advisers)

The Commission proposes corresponding rules under the Investment Advisers Act of 1940 (15 U.S.C. § 80b-1 et seq.) applicable to registered investment advisers. The proposed adviser rule is substantially parallel to the proposed broker-dealer rule.

### C. Core Requirements

Both proposed rules would require covered firms using covered technologies in investor interactions to:

**1. Evaluate for Conflicts**

Covered firms must evaluate their use of covered technologies in investor interactions to determine whether such use involves or results in a conflict of interest that places the financial or other interest of the firm or an associated person ahead of the investor's interests.

The evaluation requirement is ongoing: covered firms must not only evaluate covered technologies before deployment but also conduct periodic re-evaluations and evaluate covered technologies after material changes.

Key elements of the evaluation requirement:
- The evaluation must be specific to each covered technology used in investor interactions;
- The evaluation must consider how the covered technology interacts with other systems, incentives, and practices at the firm;
- The evaluation must be documented;
- The evaluation should consider whether the covered technology's optimization objective (what it is designed to maximize) is aligned with investor interests.

**2. Eliminate or Neutralize Conflicts**

If the evaluation identifies a conflict of interest, the covered firm must either:

(a) **Eliminate** the conflict of interest (e.g., by modifying the covered technology so that it no longer optimizes for the conflicting objective, or by discontinuing use of the conflicted covered technology); or

(b) **Neutralize** the effect of the conflict of interest (e.g., by implementing controls that prevent the conflicted output from being acted upon, or by providing offsetting human oversight that ensures investor interests are protected).

The Commission has proposed the requirement to eliminate or neutralize—rather than merely disclose—based on its determination that disclosure alone is insufficient to protect investors from AI-related conflicts of interest. An investor cannot meaningfully evaluate or consent to conflicts embedded in opaque algorithmic systems that may systematically and subtly act against investor interests.

**Prohibition on Disclosure as Sole Remedy**: The proposed rules specifically provide that a covered firm may not rely solely on disclosure to satisfy the requirement to eliminate or neutralize conflicts of interest under the proposed rule.

**3. Written Policies and Procedures**

Covered firms must establish, maintain, and enforce written policies and procedures reasonably designed to:

(a) Identify all covered technologies used in investor interactions;

(b) Evaluate covered technologies for conflicts of interest prior to initial deployment and periodically thereafter;

(c) Ensure that conflicts are eliminated or neutralized;

(d) Provide for ongoing monitoring of covered technologies in investor interactions;

(e) Address changes to covered technologies that may introduce new or different conflicts;

(f) Include training for associated persons regarding the firm's obligations under the proposed rule and the firm's specific covered technologies; and

(g) Designate individuals responsible for implementing and overseeing the policies and procedures.

### D. Recordkeeping Requirements

The proposed rules include recordkeeping requirements. Covered firms must make and keep records of:

- All covered technologies used in investor interactions, including a description of each technology and its objectives;
- Evaluations of covered technologies for conflicts of interest, including the methodology and conclusions;
- Documentation of actions taken to eliminate or neutralize conflicts;
- Written policies and procedures required by the rule;
- Testing and monitoring records related to covered technologies.

Records must be maintained for a period of five years from the date of the record (three years in an accessible place), consistent with applicable SEC recordkeeping requirements.

## III. Scope and Application

### A. Scope of "Covered Technology"

The Commission's proposed definition of "covered technology" is broad and principles-based, capturing:

- Machine learning models (supervised, unsupervised, reinforcement learning);
- Deep learning and neural network models;
- Natural language processing (NLP) models;
- Large language models (LLMs) used in investor-facing contexts;
- Recommendation engines and collaborative filtering systems;
- Optimization algorithms used in portfolio construction;
- Behavioral analytics and predictive behavioral models;
- Scoring and ranking algorithms used to prioritize investor outreach;
- AI-powered chatbots and virtual assistants used in investor interactions.

The definition does not include technologies that are not used in investor interactions, or tools used solely for back-office or non-investor-facing purposes.

### B. Scope of "Investor Interactions"

The proposed rule applies when covered technologies are used in or in connection with:

- Recommendations of securities, investment strategies, account types, or financial products to investors;
- Provision of investment information, research, or commentary to investors;
- Marketing, solicitation, and advertising communications directed to specific investors based on PDA targeting;
- Account management, portfolio monitoring, and rebalancing services;
- Customer service interactions involving investment information or account services.

### C. Relation to Other Obligations

**Regulation Best Interest (Reg BI)**: The proposed rule supplements but does not replace Reg BI's best interest obligation for broker-dealers. Reg BI requires broker-dealers to act in the retail customer's best interest at the time of a recommendation, including having a reasonable basis to believe the recommendation is in the customer's best interest, disclosing material conflicts, and having policies and procedures to identify and mitigate material conflicts. The proposed rule adds an affirmative obligation to evaluate and eliminate or neutralize AI-related conflicts that may exist even absent a specific recommendation.

**Investment Adviser Fiduciary Duty**: The proposed rule supplements the general fiduciary duty applicable to investment advisers under the Investment Advisers Act. Registered investment advisers owe a duty of loyalty and a duty of care to their clients. The duty of loyalty requires advisers to act in their clients' best interests and to avoid conflicts of interest, or to fully disclose them and obtain informed client consent. The proposed rule adds a specific conflict identification, elimination, and neutralization framework applicable to PDA.

**Form CRS**: Form CRS (Customer Relationship Summary) requires broker-dealers and investment advisers to describe conflicts of interest in plain language. The proposed rule does not change Form CRS requirements, but firms subject to the proposed rule will need to ensure their Form CRS accurately reflects their AI-related conflicts.

## IV. Economic Analysis

### A. Benefits

The Commission identified the following potential benefits of the proposed rules:

1. **Investor protection**: Requiring firms to identify, eliminate, and neutralize AI-related conflicts of interest should reduce instances where PDA is used against investor interests.

2. **Market integrity**: Ensuring that AI in financial services is used in a manner consistent with investor interests supports broader confidence in financial markets.

3. **Level playing field**: Establishing clear requirements for all covered firms should prevent a "race to the bottom" in which firms compete by deploying more aggressively conflicted AI.

4. **Transparency and accountability**: Documentation and recordkeeping requirements will support regulatory oversight of AI-related conflicts.

### B. Costs

The Commission acknowledged that the proposed rules would impose compliance costs on covered firms, including:

- Costs of evaluating covered technologies for conflicts of interest;
- Costs of modifying or discontinuing covered technologies that present conflicts;
- Costs of developing, implementing, and enforcing written policies and procedures;
- Costs of recordkeeping;
- Potential costs from limitations on the use of PDA in investor interactions.

The Commission estimated that larger broker-dealers and investment advisers that already use sophisticated PDA systems would face higher compliance costs, while smaller firms using more limited analytical tools may face lower costs.

### C. Market Structure Effects

The Commission considered potential effects of the proposed rules on innovation and competition in financial technology. The Commission expressed the view that rules that ensure AI is used consistently with investor interests — rather than restricting AI use categorically — should support continued beneficial innovation while preventing harmful applications.

## V. Key Issues and Comment Themes

The comment period for the proposed rules generated substantial public feedback. Key themes from commenters included:

### A. Definition of "Covered Technology"

Many commenters argued that the proposed definition of "covered technology" is overly broad, potentially capturing simple analytical tools that pose no meaningful conflict risk. Several commenters urged the Commission to narrow the definition to focus on technologies that actually optimize for firm interests.

Proponents of a broad definition argued that a technology-neutral, principles-based definition is necessary to prevent evasion and to remain applicable as AI technologies evolve.

### B. "Eliminate or Neutralize" Standard

Significant controversy arose over the Commission's proposed requirement to eliminate or neutralize conflicts, rather than permitting disclosure as an alternative. Many broker-dealers and investment advisers argued that:

- Disclosure is sufficient to protect sophisticated investors;
- The "eliminate or neutralize" requirement will chill beneficial uses of AI in investor services;
- It is unclear what conduct constitutes adequate "neutralization" of a conflict.

Consumer advocacy groups generally supported the "eliminate or neutralize" requirement, arguing that disclosure alone is insufficient to protect retail investors from opaque algorithmic conflicts.

### C. Definitional Clarity

Commenters requested additional guidance on:

- What analytical tools qualify as "covered technologies";
- What constitutes an "investor interaction" subject to the rule;
- How firms should evaluate AI systems for conflicts of interest where the AI's decision logic is not fully interpretable;
- Whether third-party AI systems licensed from vendors are subject to the rule.

### D. Interaction with Existing Obligations

Commenters sought clarification on how the proposed rules interact with:

- Regulation Best Interest;
- The investment adviser fiduciary duty;
- FINRA rules applicable to broker-dealers;
- State investment adviser regulations.

---

## VI. Implementation Timeline

The proposed rules were released for comment on July 26, 2023. The public comment period closed on October 10, 2023. As of the date of this document, the Commission has received comments and is in the process of reviewing them. Final rules have not yet been adopted.

The Commission indicated that, following adoption, covered firms would be provided a transition period to implement required policies and procedures. The length of the transition period has not been determined but the Commission indicated it would take into account the complexity of compliance, firm size, and the nature of covered technologies in use.

---

## VII. Relation to Other Federal AI Initiatives

The proposed rules are consistent with the Biden Administration's Executive Order on AI Safety and Security (October 2023), which directed federal financial regulators to evaluate the use of AI in financial services and develop appropriate guidance and rules.

The Commission also noted alignment with:
- NIST AI Risk Management Framework (2023) — risk identification and governance principles;
- Financial Stability Oversight Council (FSOC) guidance on AI risk in financial stability;
- International regulatory developments including the EU AI Act's classification of AI used in financial services.
