## Summary and Analysis

### Purpose and Scope

Undang-Undang Nomor 27 Tahun 2022 tentang Pelindungan Data Pribadi (Law No. 27 of 2022 on Personal Data Protection, "UU PDP") is Indonesia's first comprehensive personal data protection statute, enacted on 17 October 2022 after years of legislative development. Prior to UU PDP, Indonesia's data protection framework consisted of scattered provisions in sectoral laws and Government Regulation No. 71 of 2019 on Electronic Systems and Transactions (PP 71/2019), which provided only basic data protection principles. UU PDP establishes a modern, comprehensive framework drawing on the GDPR and the personal data protection laws of neighboring ASEAN countries including Singapore and Thailand.

UU PDP applies to any processing of personal data by any natural or legal person, public authority, or other body, whether inside or outside Indonesia, where: the processing is carried out in Indonesia; or the processing relates to Indonesian citizens or residents regardless of where processing takes place (extraterritorial application). The Law covers all forms of processing including collection, recording, storing, organizing, maintaining, keeping, modifying, obtaining, reading, displaying, disclosing, transmitting, distributing, using, deleting, or destroying personal data.

The Law applies to both private entities and government bodies, though government processing for national security, law enforcement, and intelligence purposes is subject to separate sectoral regulation. The Law imposes differentiated obligations based on the risk profile of processing, with enhanced requirements for entities that process large volumes of data or sensitive personal data. A **2-year implementation period** was provided, meaning full compliance obligations came into effect in October 2024.

The **National Data Protection Authority (NDPA)** — established as an independent body under Presidential authority with the working title Badan Pelindungan Data Pribadi — is the supervisory authority responsible for administration, enforcement, and international cooperation. The NDPA is empowered to issue implementing regulations, receive and investigate complaints, conduct audits, and impose administrative penalties.

### Key Definitions

**Personal data** (data pribadi) means any data about an identified or identifiable natural person, whether a citizen or permanent resident of Indonesia.

**Specific personal data** (data pribadi yang bersifat spesifik) — equivalent to sensitive personal data under GDPR — is defined as: health data; biometric data; genetic data; criminal records; child data; personal financial data; and other data as determined by applicable law.

**General personal data** (data pribadi yang bersifat umum) means personal data that does not fall within the specific categories, including name, gender, nationality, religion, and address.

**Personal data controller** (pengendali data pribadi) means any natural or legal person, public authority, or other body that alone or jointly with others determines the purposes and means of processing personal data.

**Personal data processor** (prosesor data pribadi) means any natural or legal person, public authority, or body that processes personal data on behalf of and under the instruction of a controller.

**Consent** (persetujuan) must be explicit (for specific personal data), free, specific, informed, and unambiguous. For general personal data, consent may be either explicit or implied through clear affirmative action. Consent must be documented and revocable at any time.

### Core Obligations for Data Controllers and Processors

**Lawful Processing Grounds.** UU PDP requires that processing of personal data be based on at least one of the following legal bases: valid and explicit consent; performance of a contractual obligation; fulfillment of a legal obligation; protection of vital interests; performance of a public service task; and/or fulfillment of other legitimate interests of the controller, provided that the interests of the data subject are not overridden.

For **specific personal data**, processing requires explicit consent and is subject to enhanced security and handling requirements. The transfer of specific personal data to third parties is subject to additional restrictions.

**Transparency and Privacy Notices.** Before processing or at the time of collection, controllers must provide data subjects with a comprehensive privacy notice containing: the identity and contact details of the controller; the legal basis and purpose of processing; the categories of personal data being processed; the intended recipients and any international transfers; the retention period; the data subject's rights; and, where applicable, the existence of automated decision-making.

**Data Minimization and Purpose Limitation.** Controllers must collect only the personal data necessary for the specified purpose and must not process data beyond that purpose. Personal data must be accurate and kept up to date as necessary.

**Security Obligations.** Controllers must implement appropriate technical and organizational security measures commensurate with the nature and risks of the processing. Security measures must protect personal data from unauthorized access, modification, disclosure, destruction, or loss. Controllers are required to conduct periodic security risk assessments and to document their security measures.

**Data Breach Notification.** Controllers must notify the NDPA and affected data subjects of personal data breaches that pose a risk to data subjects' rights and interests. Notification to the NDPA must occur within 14 days (calendar days) of discovery of the breach, and notification to affected data subjects must be provided as soon as practicable.

**Data Protection Officer (DPO).** Controllers and processors that: (a) carry out processing on behalf of the government; (b) carry out processing activities that require large-scale, regular, and systematic monitoring of data subjects; or (c) process specific personal data at large scale, must appoint a DPO. The DPO may be a natural person (including an employee) or an external service provider. The DPO's functions include advising on compliance, monitoring adherence to the Law, coordinating with the NDPA, and conducting internal audits.

**Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs).** Where processing is likely to result in high risk to data subjects' rights and interests — including processing using new technologies, large-scale processing of specific personal data, or systematic profiling producing significant effects — controllers must conduct a DPIA prior to commencing processing.

**Records of Processing Activities.** Controllers must maintain comprehensive records of their processing activities, including purposes, categories of data and data subjects, recipients, international transfers, retention periods, and security measures.

**Processor Agreements.** Controllers must engage processors only pursuant to a written agreement specifying the scope, nature, and purpose of processing, the type of personal data and data subjects, and the obligations and rights of the controller. Processors may not sub-process without prior written authorization from the controller.

**Data Localization and Residency.** A significant feature of UU PDP is its data localization requirement. The Law requires that controllers conducting activities using electronic systems in Indonesia store personal data on servers located in Indonesia. This requirement applies to electronic system operators providing public services. Where a controller also stores data abroad, it must ensure that the servers in Indonesia contain the same data. The localization requirement has significant implications for cloud computing and cross-border data flows.

### Rights of Data Subjects

UU PDP provides **10 enumerated rights** for data subjects, representing one of the most comprehensive codifications of data subject rights in ASEAN:

1. **Right to information** about the controller's identity, legal basis, purpose, and categories of data being processed.
2. **Right of access** to personal data being processed and to obtain a copy.
3. **Right to correction** of inaccurate, incomplete, or outdated personal data.
4. **Right to erasure** ("right to be forgotten") where data is no longer needed, consent is withdrawn, or processing is unlawful.
5. **Right to restriction** of processing while accuracy is contested or an objection is considered.
6. **Right to portability** — to receive personal data in a structured, commonly used, machine-readable format and to transmit it to another controller.
7. **Right to object** to processing based on legitimate interests or for direct marketing.
8. **Right against automated decision-making** — not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing that produce legal or similarly significant effects.
9. **Right to withdraw consent** at any time without detriment to lawfulness of prior processing.
10. **Right to file a complaint** with the NDPA and to seek civil compensation for violations.

Controllers must respond to data subject requests within 30 days. Requests may be refused on limited grounds, and refusals must be communicated to the data subject with reasons and information about further remedies.

### Automated Processing and Profiling Provisions

UU PDP includes dedicated provisions addressing automated processing and profiling, reflecting Indonesia's recognition of the growing role of AI in commercial and governmental decision-making.

Data subjects have the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing — including profiling — that produces legal effects or similarly significant effects. Automated decisions in areas such as employment screening, credit assessment, insurance pricing, and access to public services are subject to this right.

Exceptions apply where the automated decision: is necessary for the performance of a contract; is required by law; or is made with the data subject's explicit consent. In each permitted case, the controller must: inform the data subject that an automated decision was made; provide meaningful information about the automated decision-making logic; allow the data subject to express their view; allow the data subject to contest the decision; and provide for human review of the decision.

Controllers using automated processing or profiling must conduct a DPIA and must implement appropriate safeguards against discriminatory or erroneous outcomes. The NDPA is empowered to issue sector-specific guidance on automated processing, particularly for financial services, employment, and public administration.

### Cross-Border Transfer Rules

UU PDP establishes a framework for international transfers that must be balanced against the localization requirements described above. Personal data may be transferred outside Indonesia only where:

- The recipient country is assessed by the government to have data protection standards equivalent to Indonesia's;
- The controller has entered into a data transfer agreement imposing binding obligations on the recipient equivalent to UU PDP's requirements; or
- A specific derogation applies, such as explicit consent or necessity for vital interests.

Controllers must report international data transfers to the NDPA and must ensure that transferred data continues to be protected to the standard required by UU PDP throughout its lifecycle. For data that is subject to localization requirements, international transfer does not exempt the controller from also maintaining a copy in Indonesia.

### Enforcement and Penalties

The NDPA has broad investigatory and enforcement powers, including the ability to conduct inspections, require production of documents, issue compliance orders, suspend processing, and impose administrative fines. The NDPA may also coordinate with sector-specific regulators including OJK (financial services), Kominfo (telecommunications), and BPJS (social insurance).

UU PDP establishes significant financial penalties:

**Administrative penalties** for violations of the Law's requirements can reach up to **2% of annual revenue** of the controller in Indonesia. The NDPA determines penalties taking into account the nature, severity, and duration of the violation; the number of data subjects affected; and the measures taken to mitigate harm. Controllers may also be ordered to delete or destroy unlawfully processed personal data and to bear the costs of notification to data subjects.

**Criminal penalties** apply to the most serious violations:
- Intentional and unauthorized collection of personal data for personal benefit or to harm data subjects: imprisonment up to **5 years** and/or fines up to **Rp 5,000,000,000** (approximately USD 320,000).
- Intentional and unauthorized disclosure of personal data for personal benefit or to harm data subjects: imprisonment up to **4 years** and/or fines up to **Rp 4,000,000,000**.
- Forgery of personal data for personal benefit or to harm others: imprisonment up to **6 years** and/or fines up to **Rp 6,000,000,000**.

For **corporations**, the maximum fine is multiplied tenfold: up to **Rp 50,000,000,000** (approximately USD 3.2 million) and up to **Rp 2,000,000,000,000** (approximately USD 130 million, equivalent to approximately **Rp 2 trillion**) for the most serious corporate violations. The Rp 2 trillion figure referenced in UU PDP represents the ceiling for corporate criminal fines.

### Implementation and Transition

UU PDP provided a 2-year implementation period from its enactment in October 2022, with full compliance obligations taking effect in October 2024. During this transition period, existing regulations under the Electronic Information and Transactions Law (UU ITE) and Government Regulation 71/2019 continued to apply. The Indonesian government used the transition period to establish the NDPA, draft implementing regulations, and develop guidance for key sectors. Controllers were expected to complete compliance audits, update contracts with processors and partners, review and update privacy policies, appoint DPOs where required, and prepare DPIA templates for high-risk processing activities.
