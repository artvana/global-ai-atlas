EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Artificial Intelligence (AI) refers to the capability of algorithms integrated into systems and tools to learn from data so that they can perform automated tasks without explicit programming of every step by a human. Generative AI is a category of AI techniques in which algorithms are trained on data sets that can be used to generate new content, such as text, images or video.

This guidance addresses one type of generative AI, large multi-modal models (LMMs), which can accept one or more type of data input and generate diverse outputs that are not limited to the type of data fed into the algorithm. It has been predicted that LMMs will have wide use and application in health care, scientific research, public health and drug development. LMMs are also known as "general-purpose foundation models", although it is not yet proven whether LMMs can accomplish a wide range of tasks and purposes.

LMMs have been adopted faster than any consumer application in history. They are compelling because they facilitate human-computer interaction to mimic human communication and to generate responses to queries or data inputs that may appear human-like and authoritative. With rapid consumer adoption and uptake and in view of its potential to disrupt core social services and economic sectors, many large technology companies, start-ups and governments are investing in and competing to guide the development of generative AI.

In 2021, WHO published comprehensive guidance on the ethics and governance of AI for health. WHO consulted 20 leading experts in AI, who identified both potential benefits and potential risks of use of AI in health care and issued six principles arrived at by consensus for consideration in the policies and practices of governments, developers, and providers that are using AI. The principles should guide the development and deployment of AI in health care.

The six principles are:
1. Protecting human autonomy
2. Promoting human well-being and safety and the public interest
3. Ensuring transparency, explainability and intelligibility
4. Fostering responsibility and accountability
5. Ensuring inclusiveness and equity
6. Promoting AI that is responsive and sustainable

This guidance on LMMs builds on those six principles and the need to address the specific risks and challenges that LMMs pose for health.

Key areas addressed in this guidance:

Diagnosis and clinical care: LMMs are increasingly being used in radiology, pathology, ophthalmology, and dermatology for image analysis and interpretation. They can also support clinical decision-making, documentation, and communication with patients.

Patient-centred applications: LMMs can support patients in understanding their health conditions, treatment options, and healthcare navigation. Mental health applications using LMMs raise particular concerns about safety, privacy, and the nature of therapeutic relationships.

Administrative tasks: LMMs can automate documentation, prior authorization, and administrative communications, potentially reducing clinician burden and improving efficiency.

Medical education: LMMs can simulate patient interactions, provide feedback to medical students, and support self-directed learning. However, concerns exist about accuracy of medical information provided.

Research and drug development: LMMs may accelerate drug discovery, literature synthesis, and clinical trial design.

Key risks identified:

- Hallucinations and inaccuracies: LMMs can generate plausible-sounding but incorrect medical information, posing significant patient safety risks in clinical settings.
- Bias: LMMs trained on non-representative data can perpetuate or amplify health disparities.
- Privacy: LMMs require large amounts of training data, raising concerns about patient data privacy.
- Opacity: The inner workings of LMMs may be difficult to explain, complicating clinical accountability.
- Overreliance: Clinicians and patients may over-trust LMM outputs.
- Environmental impact: Training and running LMMs consumes significant energy and computational resources.
- Regulatory challenges: Existing medical device regulations may not be well-suited to governing LMMs that can be updated or repurposed.
- Inequity: Access to LMM-based healthcare applications may be limited to high-income settings.

Key recommendations (summary):

For developers:
- Conduct thorough pre-market testing across diverse populations
- Provide transparent documentation of training data, limitations, and intended uses
- Implement safeguards against harmful outputs in health contexts
- Establish mechanisms for post-market surveillance
- Engage with health professionals and patients in design and evaluation

For governments:
- Develop regulatory frameworks specifically addressing generative AI in healthcare
- Require pre-market evaluation and ongoing post-market surveillance
- Mandate transparency about when patients are interacting with AI
- Establish national data governance frameworks
- Invest in AI literacy for health professionals

For providers/deployers:
- Ensure clear informed consent processes when AI is used in care
- Maintain human oversight and clinical accountability
- Monitor AI system performance in their specific patient populations
- Develop policies on appropriate use of LMMs by clinicians

On liability:
- Establish clear frameworks for allocating liability among developers, providers, and deployers
- Recognize that existing legal frameworks may need updating to address AI-specific scenarios

On international governance:
- WHO calls for international coordination on standards, evaluation methods, and regulatory approaches
- Developing countries need support to benefit from and govern LMMs in health
