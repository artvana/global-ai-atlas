# ASEAN Guide on Artificial Intelligence Governance and Ethics (2nd Edition, 2023)

*Published by the ASEAN Digital Ministers' Meeting (ADGMIN), February 2023. Voluntary framework for the 10 ASEAN Member States: Brunei Darussalam, Cambodia, Indonesia, Lao PDR, Malaysia, Myanmar, Philippines, Singapore, Thailand, and Viet Nam.*

---

## Foreword

The ASEAN Guide on AI Governance and Ethics (2nd Edition) builds on the first edition published in 2021, incorporating feedback from industry, government, and academia across the region. The guide provides practical guidance for organizations in both the public and private sectors on implementing responsible AI, aligned with the ASEAN AI Principles adopted in 2019. This second edition expands coverage to include new use cases in healthcare, financial services, and smart cities, and introduces guidance on generative AI and foundation models.

---

## Part I: Overview and Purpose

### 1.1 Purpose and Scope

This guide is intended to:

- Assist organizations in the ASEAN region in implementing AI in a responsible, trustworthy, and human-centric manner;
- Provide a common framework that is compatible with international standards while reflecting ASEAN-specific values and development contexts;
- Enable organizations to adopt AI with appropriate governance structures proportionate to the risk level of the AI system; and
- Facilitate cross-border AI cooperation among ASEAN Member States.

The guide applies to organizations developing, deploying, and using AI systems, including AI-enabled products and services. It is voluntary and does not override any applicable national laws or regulations.

### 1.2 Definitions

For purposes of this guide:

- **Artificial Intelligence (AI) System**: A machine-based system that can, for a given set of objectives, make predictions, recommendations, or decisions influencing real or virtual environments.
- **AI Governance**: Policies, processes, and structures put in place to ensure that AI systems are designed, developed, and deployed in a responsible manner.
- **Algorithmic Decision-Making**: The use of algorithms to automate or assist decisions that have an impact on individuals or organizations.
- **High-Risk AI**: AI systems used in contexts where errors or failures could have significant adverse consequences for individuals, groups, or society, including AI used in healthcare, law enforcement, financial services, employment, and critical infrastructure.
- **Foundation Model / Large Language Model (LLM)**: A large AI model trained on broad data at scale that can be adapted to a wide range of downstream tasks.

### 1.3 Relationship to International Frameworks

The guide is aligned with and complementary to:

- OECD Principles on AI (2019, updated 2023)
- UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021)
- G20 AI Principles
- ISO/IEC AI standards, including ISO/IEC 42001 (AI Management Systems)
- Singapore's Model AI Governance Framework (2nd edition)

## Part II: The Seven ASEAN AI Principles

The following seven principles form the foundation of responsible AI governance in ASEAN. They are drawn from the ASEAN AI Principles agreed upon by ASEAN Digital Ministers in 2019 and reaffirmed in subsequent ministerial meetings.

### Principle 1: Transparency and Explainability

AI systems should be transparent and explainable to the extent possible.

**Guidance:**
- Organizations should be able to explain how AI-driven decisions are made, particularly decisions that affect individuals or have significant consequences.
- Users should be informed when they are interacting with an AI system or receiving AI-generated output.
- Organizations should document the purpose, data inputs, model architecture, training methodology, and known limitations of AI systems.
- Where full technical explainability is not feasible (e.g., complex neural networks), organizations should at minimum provide meaningful, contextually relevant explanations of AI-assisted decisions.
- Explanations provided to affected individuals should be in plain language, free from unnecessary technical jargon, and actionable.

### Principle 2: Fairness and Equity

AI systems should be fair and should not discriminate against individuals or groups on the basis of protected characteristics such as race, gender, age, disability, religion, or national origin.

**Guidance:**
- Organizations should assess AI systems for potential discriminatory impacts prior to deployment and on an ongoing basis.
- Training data should be examined for historical biases that may be perpetuated or amplified by the AI system.
- Organizations should use diverse and representative datasets and engage diverse teams in AI development.
- Where algorithmic bias is identified, organizations should take corrective action, which may include re-training the model, adjusting decision thresholds, or implementing human oversight mechanisms.
- Fairness metrics should be defined, documented, and regularly monitored. Organizations should be aware that different fairness definitions may be mathematically incompatible and should clearly state which definition is applied and why.

### Principle 3: Security and Safety

AI systems should be designed to be robust and secure throughout their lifecycle.

**Guidance:**
- Organizations should conduct risk assessments prior to deploying AI systems, identifying potential failure modes, adversarial attacks, data poisoning risks, and unintended behaviors.
- AI systems should be tested across a wide range of conditions, including edge cases and adversarial scenarios, before deployment.
- Organizations should maintain the ability to override, correct, or shut down AI systems as needed (fail-safe mechanisms).
- Cybersecurity measures appropriate to the sensitivity of the data processed and the criticality of the AI system's function should be implemented.
- AI systems should degrade gracefully: when certainty is low, systems should flag uncertainty rather than proceed with low-confidence decisions.
- Organizations operating AI in critical infrastructure should conduct regular red-team exercises and penetration testing.

### Principle 4: Human-Centricity and Oversight

AI should be human-centric, with meaningful human oversight maintained, particularly for high-stakes decisions.

**Guidance:**
- Human-in-the-loop mechanisms should be implemented for high-risk AI applications, ensuring that consequential decisions are subject to human review.
- AI systems should augment human capabilities rather than replace human judgment in high-stakes contexts.
- The degree of human oversight should be proportionate to the potential impact of the AI system's decisions: fully automated decisions may be appropriate for low-risk, high-volume tasks, while human review is essential for decisions affecting life, liberty, employment, and access to essential services.
- Organizations should establish clear lines of responsibility for AI-assisted decisions and train personnel on AI system capabilities and limitations.
- Individuals affected by AI-assisted decisions should have access to meaningful recourse, including the ability to request human review.

### Principle 5: Data Governance and Privacy

AI systems should comply with applicable data protection laws and privacy principles.

**Guidance:**
- Data collected and used for AI training and operation should be subject to the same privacy protections as other personal data.
- Organizations should implement data minimization principles: collecting only the data necessary for the AI system's function.
- Consent mechanisms should be clear and meaningful, with individuals understanding what data is being collected, how it will be used, and the role of AI in processing.
- Organizations should implement strong data governance frameworks covering data quality, data lineage, access controls, retention periods, and deletion.
- Transfer of personal data used in AI systems across ASEAN Member States should comply with applicable cross-border data transfer rules.
- Privacy Impact Assessments (PIAs) should be conducted for AI systems processing sensitive personal data.

### Principle 6: Accountability and Integrity

Organizations developing and deploying AI are accountable for the AI systems they create and the outcomes those systems produce.

**Guidance:**
- Organizations should designate individuals or teams with responsibility for AI governance and oversight (e.g., an AI Ethics Officer or AI Review Board).
- Governance documentation—including risk assessments, fairness evaluations, testing records, and incident reports—should be maintained and available for audit.
- Organizations should have incident response procedures for AI-related harms, including processes for remediation and communication to affected parties.
- Supply chain accountability is important: organizations that deploy third-party AI systems or models should conduct due diligence on their AI vendors and understand the risks they introduce.
- AI governance should be integrated into existing enterprise risk management and corporate governance frameworks.
- Organizations should not use AI to circumvent legal obligations, manipulate individuals, or engage in deceptive practices.

### Principle 7: Technology and Ecosystem Reliability

AI systems should be reliable, valid, and reproducible. The broader AI ecosystem should be trustworthy.

**Guidance:**
- AI systems should perform consistently and predictably within their intended domain. Performance should be measured against clearly defined benchmarks.
- Organizations should monitor AI system performance post-deployment and implement processes for continuous improvement.
- Model drift (degradation in AI performance as data patterns change over time) should be monitored and addressed.
- Organizations should maintain version control for AI models and document changes to models in production.
- Collaboration within the AI ecosystem—including between AI developers, deployers, researchers, regulators, civil society, and the public—is essential to building trust in AI.

## Part III: AI Governance Framework for Organizations

### 3.1 Tiered Risk-Based Approach

The guide recommends a tiered, risk-based approach to AI governance, recognizing that not all AI systems require the same level of scrutiny.

**Tier 1 – High-Risk AI:**
AI systems where failures or errors could have serious consequences including harm to life, health, safety, or fundamental rights. Examples include:
- AI used in medical diagnosis or treatment
- AI used in autonomous vehicles
- AI used in criminal justice, law enforcement, or border control
- AI used in hiring and employment decisions affecting large numbers of people
- AI used in allocating financial credit, insurance, or essential services

*Governance requirements for Tier 1:* Mandatory pre-deployment impact assessment; human-in-the-loop for consequential decisions; mandatory explainability to affected individuals; regular third-party audits; post-deployment monitoring; documented incident response procedures.

**Tier 2 – Medium-Risk AI:**
AI systems that affect significant numbers of people or make decisions with moderate consequences but do not pose risks to life or fundamental rights. Examples include:
- AI-powered customer service chatbots
- AI-based recommendation systems in e-commerce
- AI used in marketing targeting
- AI used in HR screening (non-final stage)

*Governance requirements for Tier 2:* Impact assessment recommended; human oversight mechanisms; transparency to users about AI involvement; fairness assessments; internal audit and review.

**Tier 3 – Low-Risk AI:**
AI systems used in low-stakes contexts with limited impact on individuals. Examples include:
- AI used for spam filtering
- AI used for content categorization
- AI used for process automation in back-office functions

*Governance requirements for Tier 3:* Basic documentation; general compliance with data governance standards; periodic review.

### 3.2 AI Impact Assessment

Organizations should conduct AI Impact Assessments (AIAs) for medium- and high-risk AI systems. An AIA should address:

1. **Purpose and use case**: What is the AI system designed to do? Who will use it? In what contexts?
2. **Data assessment**: What data is used to train and operate the system? Is the data representative, accurate, and collected lawfully?
3. **Bias and fairness assessment**: Are there potential discriminatory outcomes? How will these be measured and mitigated?
4. **Privacy assessment**: What personal data is processed? Does use comply with applicable data protection law?
5. **Safety and security assessment**: What are the potential failure modes? What safeguards are in place?
6. **Human oversight assessment**: What level of human review is built into the system? How are contested decisions escalated?
7. **Explainability assessment**: Can the AI system's decisions be explained to affected individuals? If not, what alternative accountability mechanisms are in place?
8. **Ongoing monitoring plan**: How will the system's performance and impacts be monitored post-deployment?

### 3.3 AI Documentation and Model Cards

Organizations should maintain comprehensive documentation for AI systems, including:

- **Model Cards**: Standardized documentation describing the AI model's purpose, training data, performance metrics, known limitations, and recommended uses and misuses.
- **Data Sheets for Datasets**: Documentation of datasets used in AI training, including provenance, collection methods, preprocessing steps, and known biases.
- **System Cards**: For complex AI systems consisting of multiple models or components, a system-level description of how components interact.

### 3.4 Human Oversight Mechanisms

For high-risk AI systems, organizations should implement one or more of the following human oversight mechanisms:

- **Human-in-the-loop (HITL)**: A human reviews and approves AI recommendations before any decision is implemented.
- **Human-on-the-loop (HOTL)**: The AI system operates autonomously but a human monitors its decisions and can intervene.
- **Human-in-command (HIC)**: Humans retain the overall authority to override, modify, or shut down the AI system at any time.

The appropriate mechanism depends on the speed requirements of the decision, the consequences of error, and available human resources.

### 3.5 Incident Response and Reporting

Organizations should establish AI incident response procedures covering:

1. **Detection**: Processes for identifying AI-related harms, including user feedback channels, automated monitoring, and regular audits.
2. **Assessment**: Evaluating the severity and scope of the incident, including number of affected individuals and nature of harm.
3. **Containment**: Temporarily disabling or modifying the AI system to prevent further harm.
4. **Remediation**: Addressing the root cause of the incident, which may involve retraining the model, correcting data errors, or revising governance procedures.
5. **Communication**: Notifying affected individuals and, where required by law, relevant regulators.
6. **Post-incident review**: Documenting lessons learned and updating governance procedures accordingly.

## Part IV: Sector-Specific Guidance

### 4.1 Healthcare

- AI used in clinical decision support should be subject to regulatory approval where required by national law.
- AI should not replace physician judgment in diagnosis or treatment; it should serve as a decision aid.
- Patient data used to train AI systems must be handled in compliance with applicable health data privacy laws and ethical standards.
- AI-generated diagnoses or recommendations presented to clinicians should include confidence intervals and known limitations.
- Patients should be informed when AI is used in their care in a clinically significant way.
- Post-market surveillance of AI-powered medical devices is essential.

### 4.2 Financial Services

- AI used in credit scoring, insurance underwriting, and loan origination should be explainable to affected consumers.
- Lenders and insurers using AI should be able to provide specific, actionable reasons for adverse decisions.
- Anti-money laundering (AML) and fraud detection AI systems should include human review for significant decisions.
- AI systems used in algorithmic trading should include circuit breakers and other risk controls.
- Organizations should assess whether AI systems in financial services perpetuate or amplify existing biases against protected groups.

### 4.3 Employment and Human Resources

- AI used in recruitment, performance management, or termination decisions should be subject to rigorous fairness assessments.
- Job candidates and employees should be informed when AI is used in employment decisions.
- Individuals should have the right to request human review of AI-assisted employment decisions.
- AI systems used in workforce monitoring should respect applicable privacy laws and employee rights.

### 4.4 Smart Cities and Public Services

- AI systems used in public service delivery should be subject to public impact assessments and, where appropriate, public consultation.
- Governments deploying AI in law enforcement, surveillance, or benefits administration should apply heightened scrutiny and oversight.
- Citizens interacting with AI-powered government services should be informed and should have access to human alternatives.
- AI systems used in public safety contexts should be regularly evaluated for accuracy and potential disparate impact on minority groups.

### 4.5 Generative AI and Foundation Models

The 2nd edition of the guide introduces specific guidance for generative AI, including large language models (LLMs), image generators, and multimodal systems.

- **Transparency**: Deployers of generative AI should clearly disclose to users that they are interacting with an AI system.
- **Content authenticity**: Organizations generating synthetic media (deepfakes, AI-generated images, voice cloning) should implement content provenance mechanisms (e.g., digital watermarking, content credentials).
- **Misinformation risks**: Organizations deploying generative AI should implement safeguards against the generation of disinformation, hate speech, and other harmful content.
- **Intellectual property**: Organizations training generative AI on third-party content should be aware of applicable copyright laws and take steps to respect rights holders.
- **Data privacy in training**: Foundation model developers should ensure that personal data used in pre-training is handled in compliance with applicable data protection laws, and should implement techniques such as differential privacy where appropriate.
- **Evaluation and red-teaming**: Before deploying foundation models or generative AI products, organizations should conduct comprehensive safety evaluations, including adversarial red-teaming to identify potential harms.
- **Downstream responsibility**: Foundation model providers should publish usage policies, safety documentation, and guidance for downstream deployers. Downstream deployers retain responsibility for the safe and responsible use of foundation models in their products.

## Part V: Implementation Guidance

### 5.1 Building an AI Governance Function

Organizations should consider establishing:

- **AI Governance Committee or Board**: Senior leadership oversight of AI strategy and risk.
- **AI Ethics Officer or AI Risk Manager**: Dedicated responsibility for implementing AI governance policies.
- **Cross-functional AI Review Process**: Involving legal, compliance, data privacy, cybersecurity, and business stakeholders in AI deployment decisions.
- **External Advisory Board**: Engagement of independent experts, civil society representatives, and affected community members.

### 5.2 Staff Training and Awareness

- All staff working with AI systems should receive training on AI risks, governance policies, and their responsibilities.
- Technical staff should receive training on bias, fairness, explainability, and security in AI development.
- Executives and board members should receive training on AI governance and the organization's AI risk profile.

### 5.3 Vendor and Third-Party Management

- Organizations that deploy AI systems developed by third parties should conduct due diligence on the AI vendor's governance practices.
- AI procurement contracts should include provisions addressing data governance, model documentation, performance standards, audit rights, and incident notification.
- Organizations should not assume that AI systems certified in one jurisdiction automatically meet the governance standards required in ASEAN markets.

### 5.4 Engagement with Regulators and Standards Bodies

- Organizations are encouraged to engage proactively with national regulators on AI governance.
- Organizations should participate in industry working groups and contribute to the development of national and regional AI standards.
- Organizations should follow developments in ISO/IEC AI standards and align internal governance practices with emerging standards where relevant.

## Part VI: ASEAN Cooperation on AI Governance

### 6.1 Cross-Border AI Governance

As AI systems increasingly operate across national borders, ASEAN Member States commit to:

- Sharing information and best practices on AI governance frameworks.
- Working toward mutual recognition of AI governance standards and certification schemes.
- Establishing mechanisms for cross-border incident reporting and response.
- Cooperating on capacity building for AI governance, particularly in developing member states.

### 6.2 ASEAN AI Governance Collaboration with International Partners

ASEAN engages with international partners including the EU, US, UK, Japan, and South Korea on AI governance alignment, with the goals of:

- Avoiding fragmentation of AI governance frameworks that could impede regional economic integration.
- Promoting interoperability of AI governance standards.
- Sharing technical expertise and capacity-building resources.

## Annexes

### Annex A: Self-Assessment Checklist for Organizations

Organizations can use this checklist to assess their current AI governance maturity:

**Accountability**
- [ ] Has the organization designated responsibility for AI governance?
- [ ] Are AI governance policies documented and communicated internally?
- [ ] Does the organization conduct AI risk assessments prior to deployment?
- [ ] Are third-party AI vendors subject to due diligence?

**Fairness**
- [ ] Has the organization assessed AI systems for potential discriminatory outcomes?
- [ ] Are training datasets reviewed for bias?
- [ ] Are fairness metrics defined and monitored?

**Transparency**
- [ ] Are users informed when they interact with AI systems?
- [ ] Can the organization explain AI-assisted decisions to affected individuals?
- [ ] Is AI system documentation (model cards, data sheets) maintained?

**Safety and Security**
- [ ] Are AI systems tested for robustness and adversarial vulnerabilities?
- [ ] Are fail-safe mechanisms in place?
- [ ] Are cybersecurity controls applied to AI systems and training data?

**Human Oversight**
- [ ] Are human oversight mechanisms proportionate to the risk level of each AI system?
- [ ] Are individuals able to request human review of AI-assisted decisions?

**Data Governance**
- [ ] Does data used in AI comply with applicable privacy laws?
- [ ] Are data governance policies documented and enforced?

**Monitoring**
- [ ] Is AI system performance monitored post-deployment?
- [ ] Are AI-related incidents tracked and reviewed?

### Annex B: Glossary of Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| Algorithmic bias | Systematic and unfair discrimination produced by an AI algorithm |
| Explainability | The degree to which the decisions of an AI system can be understood by humans |
| Fairness | The absence of inappropriate discrimination in AI-assisted decisions |
| Foundation model | A large AI model trained on broad data that can be adapted to many tasks |
| Human-in-the-loop | An oversight model where humans approve AI decisions before implementation |
| Model drift | Degradation of AI model performance over time as data patterns change |
| Red-teaming | Adversarial testing of AI systems by a team playing the role of a hostile actor |
| Risk assessment | A systematic evaluation of the potential harms associated with an AI system |
| Watermarking | A technique for embedding information in AI-generated content to indicate its provenance |
