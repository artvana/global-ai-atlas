# A Pro-Innovation Approach to AI Regulation — UK Government White Paper (CP 815)

**Department for Science, Innovation and Technology (DSIT)**
*Published 29 March 2023. Command Paper CP 815.*

---

## Overview

The UK Government's White Paper "A Pro-Innovation Approach to AI Regulation" (CP 815), published by the Department for Science, Innovation and Technology (DSIT) on 29 March 2023, sets out the UK's post-Brexit strategy for governing artificial intelligence. It represents a deliberate and explicit rejection of the EU's binding, centralised, and sector-agnostic regulatory model in favour of a flexible, sector-led, and initially non-statutory approach.

The White Paper was accompanied by a public consultation (closed 21 June 2023). The government's response to the consultation, published in February 2024, confirmed the general architecture while committing to monitoring powers for the central AI Safety Institute (AISI) and a future "backstop" legislative power if regulators fail to act.

The White Paper reflects a cross-government strategy coordinated by DSIT, the Office for AI (within DSIT), and the newly created AI Safety Institute (announced at the Bletchley Park AI Safety Summit, November 2023). It draws on the work of the Centre for Data Ethics and Innovation (CDEI), which produced the foundational "Roadmap to an Effective AI Assurance Ecosystem" (2021) that informed the White Paper's approach.

---

## Five Cross-Sector AI Principles

The White Paper's central governance mechanism is a set of five cross-sector principles, to be interpreted and applied by each UK sector regulator within their existing regulatory remit. The principles are:

### Principle 1 — Safety, Security, and Robustness

AI systems should function in a secure, safe, and robust way throughout their lifecycle. Deployers and developers should ensure AI operates as intended, can be tested and monitored, and fails safely. Regulators should consider:
- How AI systems should be tested before deployment and monitored in operation
- What security standards apply to AI systems (relevant to critical national infrastructure)
- How AI system failures should be reported and remedied

### Principle 2 — Appropriate Transparency and Explainability

Appropriate transparency about AI system functioning should be provided to regulators, to affected individuals, and to the public. The level of transparency required should be proportionate to the risk of harm. Regulators should consider:
- What information deployers of AI should provide to affected individuals
- What information developers should provide to deployers (technical documentation)
- When and how AI-generated decisions should be explained to affected persons
- How explainability obligations interact with intellectual property and commercial confidentiality

### Principle 3 — Fairness

AI systems should not be used to discriminate unlawfully against individuals or to create unfair outcomes. Regulators should consider:
- How existing protected characteristics law (Equality Act 2010) applies to AI decisions
- Whether sector-specific fairness standards are needed (e.g., in financial services, hiring, or criminal justice)
- How bias testing and fairness audits should be conducted
- The interaction between AI fairness and data protection (UK GDPR)

### Principle 4 — Accountability and Governance

There should be clear lines of accountability for AI systems and their outcomes, commensurate with the risks they present. Regulators should consider:
- How accountability should be assigned between AI developers, deployers, and integrators
- What governance requirements (board oversight, documentation, audit trails) are appropriate
- How accountability obligations interact with corporate structures including the use of third-party AI vendors

### Principle 5 — Contestability and Redress

Individuals and organisations affected by AI decisions should have appropriate mechanisms to contest those decisions and seek redress for harms. Regulators should consider:
- What redress mechanisms are available to affected individuals
- How contestability requirements apply to automated decision-making
- How regulators can investigate complaints about AI systems

## Institutional Architecture

### Role of Existing Sector Regulators

The White Paper's most distinctive feature is its reliance on existing sector regulators — rather than a new AI-specific regulator — to implement the five principles within their existing statutory mandates. Key regulators and their relevant remit:

| Regulator | Sector | Relevant AI Mandate |
|-----------|--------|---------------------|
| FCA (Financial Conduct Authority) | Financial services | Algorithmic trading, credit scoring, AI in financial advice |
| Ofcom | Broadcasting / Communications | Algorithmic content moderation, Online Safety Act |
| ICO (Information Commissioner's Office) | Data protection | UK GDPR Art. 22 automated decision-making, data protection impact assessments |
| CQC (Care Quality Commission) | Health and social care | AI in clinical decision support, medical devices |
| MHRA (Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency) | Medical devices | AI as a medical device |
| CAA (Civil Aviation Authority) | Aviation | Autonomous systems, AI in air traffic management |
| ORR (Office of Rail and Road) | Rail transport | AI in signalling and traffic management |
| HSE (Health and Safety Executive) | Workplace safety | AI in hazardous environments |
| CMA (Competition and Markets Authority) | Competition | Algorithmic pricing, market concentration in AI |

Regulators are expected to:
- Issue sector-specific guidance on how the five principles apply in their domain
- Update existing regulatory frameworks to address AI-specific issues
- Report to DSIT on their AI regulatory activities

### Central Coordination Function (DSIT/AI Safety Institute)

The White Paper establishes a central coordination function, operated by DSIT, to:
- Monitor consistency across regulators
- Identify cross-sectoral AI risks that fall between regulatory remits
- Publish a central "AI Regulatory Landscape" document
- Operate international engagement on AI governance (G7 Hiroshima AI Process, OECD, Council of Europe)

The **AI Safety Institute (AISI)**, announced at the Bletchley Park AI Safety Summit (November 2023) and operational from early 2024, is separate from but complementary to the White Paper architecture. AISI focuses specifically on frontier AI safety evaluation and is not the implementation body for the White Paper.

## Key Policy Positions

### No New AI-Specific Legislation (Initial Position)

The White Paper explicitly states that the government does not intend to introduce AI-specific legislation in the near term. The rationale:
- Existing sector regulation is sufficiently flexible to address most AI risks
- Premature legislation risks locking in requirements that do not reflect rapidly evolving technology
- A sector-led approach preserves UK competitiveness post-Brexit

*Update*: The February 2024 consultation response confirmed this position while indicating the government would introduce "backstop powers" enabling Ministers to direct regulators if they fail to implement the principles. Draft legislation for these backstop powers was under development as of May 2026.

### Proportionality and Risk-Based Application

The principles are to be applied proportionately to risk. The White Paper does not create mandatory risk tiers (unlike the EU AI Act) but instructs regulators to consider:
- The probability that an AI system causes harm
- The severity and reversibility of potential harm
- The number of individuals potentially affected
- The vulnerability of affected individuals
- Whether affected individuals gave meaningful consent

### Context-Dependence

The White Paper emphasises that AI governance must be context-dependent — the same AI system may require different governance measures depending on the sector and use case. A credit scoring model requires different governance than a music recommendation algorithm, even if both use similar underlying techniques.

### International Interoperability

The White Paper commits to ensuring the UK's AI governance approach remains internationally interoperable — particularly with the EU (AI Act), US (NIST AI RMF), and OECD AI Principles — to avoid market fragmentation and reduce compliance burdens for international businesses operating in the UK.

## Obligations and Responsibilities

### Developer Responsibilities (Indicative)

While non-statutory, the White Paper signals that developers should:
- Maintain technical documentation enabling sector regulators to assess compliance with the five principles
- Conduct risk assessments for AI systems before deployment in regulated sectors
- Provide deployers with sufficient information to understand and operate AI systems safely
- Notify relevant regulators of significant AI incidents (sector-specific)

### Deployer Responsibilities (Indicative)

Deployers in regulated sectors should:
- Assess AI systems against the five principles before deployment
- Implement appropriate oversight and monitoring
- Provide affected individuals with information about AI decision-making and accessible redress
- Cooperate with sector regulators in investigations

## Relationship to Other UK Instruments

| Instrument | Relationship |
|-----------|-------------|
| UK GDPR / Data Protection Act 2018 | Art. 22 UK GDPR governs automated decision-making rights; White Paper principles supplement but do not replace these obligations |
| Online Safety Act 2023 | Ofcom is the primary regulator for algorithmic content moderation on platforms; White Paper Principle 2 (transparency) is relevant to Ofcom's AI-related work |
| Equality Act 2010 | White Paper Principle 3 (fairness) is anchored in the Equality Act; no new protected characteristics or AI-specific fairness standards created |
| AI Safety Summit / Bletchley Declaration | International voluntary commitments on frontier AI safety; AISI founded as a follow-on |
| UK-EU TCA | Trade and Cooperation Agreement does not create AI regulatory alignment obligations; UK is free to diverge from EU AI Act |
| Council of Europe AI Convention (2024) | UK is a signatory; creates international human rights obligations for AI in public sector contexts |

## Criticisms and Limitations

- **No binding obligations**: The White Paper creates no legally enforceable AI-specific obligations; reliance on existing sector regulation leaves gaps (e.g., for AI deployed in unregulated contexts)
- **Regulatory fragmentation**: Different regulators may interpret the five principles inconsistently, creating compliance complexity for businesses operating across sectors
- **No central AI regulator**: The absence of a single AI authority means no single body has comprehensive oversight of AI-related harms
- **Limited individual rights**: No new individual rights (e.g., right to explanation, right to human review) are created by the White Paper
- **Frontier AI gap**: The White Paper does not specifically address the risks of frontier/general-purpose AI models; AISI fills part of this gap but through evaluation rather than regulation
