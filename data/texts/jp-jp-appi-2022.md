# Act on the Protection of Personal Information (APPI) — 2022 Amendments
**Act No. 57 of 2003, as amended by Act No. 77 of 2021, in force June 2022**
*Japan — In force April 1, 2022*

---

## Overview / Background

Japan's Act on the Protection of Personal Information (APPI) is the country's primary statute governing the collection, use, and management of personal data by private-sector businesses. Originally enacted in 2003, the APPI underwent a significant set of amendments in 2020 (effective October 2021) and again via Act No. 77 of 2021 (enacted June 4, 2021, fully effective April 1, 2022). The 2022 amendments represent the most expansive expansion of individual rights and organizational obligations in the APPI's history, aligning Japan more closely with international data protection norms while retaining distinctly Japanese features such as the concept of "anonymously processed information" and "pseudonymously processed information."

The Personal Information Protection Commission (PPC) is the independent government authority responsible for administering and enforcing the APPI across the private sector. The PPC may issue guidelines, conduct investigations, require reports, carry out on-site inspections, and impose administrative orders. The 2022 amendments significantly strengthened the PPC's enforcement toolkit and increased penalties for violations.

The APPI applies to all "business operators handling personal information" — broadly defined to cover any entity (individual, corporation, or organization) that uses personal information databases for business purposes. Importantly for AI applications, the APPI applies to any AI system that processes personal information of Japanese residents, regardless of whether the system operator is based in Japan.

## Key Provisions / Chapters

### Mandatory Breach Notification
- Organizations must report personal data breaches to the PPC without delay upon becoming aware of a qualifying incident.
- Incidents triggering mandatory notification include: leakage of sensitive personal information; leakage likely to cause harm to data subjects; leakage resulting from unauthorized access; and leakage affecting a large number of individuals (5,000 or more in some cases).
- In parallel, affected individuals must also be notified of qualifying breaches.
- This notification requirement did not exist under the previous version of the APPI; its introduction aligns Japan with GDPR-style breach notification norms.

### Strengthened Opt-Out Mechanism for Third-Party Provision
- The APPI's opt-out framework permits certain third-party transfers of personal information without consent, but only where the data subject has been notified and given the opportunity to object.
- The 2022 amendments tightened this mechanism: organizations wishing to transfer personal information to third parties via the opt-out route must now register with the PPC rather than merely notifying the data subject.
- Sensitive personal information categories (e.g., race, creed, medical history, criminal records, disability status) may no longer be transferred via the opt-out mechanism — explicit consent is always required.

### Right to Request Cessation of Third-Party Data Provision
- Data subjects gained a new right to demand that a business operator cease providing their personal information to third parties, regardless of whether the provision is lawful.
- This right applies where the data subject can demonstrate that continued provision would harm their interests, and reflects an expansion of individual control over personal data flows.
- Businesses must respond to such requests within a reasonable period.

### Pseudonymously Processed Information
- The 2022 amendments introduced the new category of "pseudonymously processed information" (仮名加工情報): data that has been processed so that individuals cannot be identified without reference to separately held additional information.
- Unlike "anonymously processed information," pseudonymously processed data may be retained and internally shared for analytics and research purposes with fewer restrictions, but cannot be provided to third parties under ordinary circumstances.
- This category is particularly relevant to AI model training: organizations may use pseudonymously processed data for developing and testing AI systems while retaining some reversibility for data quality purposes.

### Cross-Border Transfer Tightening
- The 2022 amendments significantly tightened requirements for cross-border transfers of personal data.
- Transfers to third countries now require either: (1) explicit opt-in consent from the data subject with full disclosure of the destination country's data protection standards; or (2) a transfer to a jurisdiction recognized by the PPC as having equivalent protections.
- The amendments introduced a new requirement that, when relying on consent, businesses must inform data subjects of the name of the receiving country and that country's data protection standards or lack thereof.
- Businesses must also confirm on an ongoing basis that recipient third parties maintain adequate protection levels.

### Expanded Individual Rights
- **Correction and Deletion:** Data subjects now have enhanced rights to request correction or deletion of inaccurate personal data and to receive timely responses.
- **Suspension of Use:** Where data is being used for purposes beyond those originally disclosed, or has been unlawfully obtained, data subjects may request that the business operator suspend use of their data.
- **Electronic Response:** Businesses must comply with data subject requests submitted electronically.
- **Third-Party Disclosure Scope:** The scope of personal data about which data subjects can request third-party disclosure records has been expanded.

### PPC On-Site Inspection Powers
- The PPC received enhanced authority to conduct on-site inspections and require businesses to submit reports on their personal data handling practices.
- Inspections may be triggered by complaints, suspected violations, or proactive regulatory review.

## Enforcement & Penalties

- **Administrative orders:** The PPC may order businesses to correct non-compliant practices, stop unlawful data handling, or take corrective measures.
- **Criminal penalties for individuals:** Individuals who unlawfully provide personal information databases to third parties face up to one year of imprisonment or a fine of up to JPY 1 million (~$6,500).
- **Corporate penalties:** Following the 2022 amendments, corporations can face fines of up to JPY 100 million (~$650,000) for violations, a tenfold increase from prior law — a significant escalation designed to deter large-scale violations.
- **Reporting offenses:** Failing to submit required reports to the PPC or making false reports is subject to criminal penalties of up to JPY 500,000.
- **PPC Guidance:** The PPC regularly issues guidelines and Q&As that carry significant practical weight; non-compliance with guidelines may lead to enforcement action.
