## Summary and Analysis

### Purpose and Scope

Proclamation No. 1321/2024, the Personal Data Protection Proclamation of Ethiopia, was enacted by the Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia and entered into force in 2024, making Ethiopia one of the first East African nations and among a growing number of African countries to adopt a comprehensive, standalone data protection statute. The Proclamation was developed against the backdrop of Ethiopia's expanding digital economy, the growth of mobile financial services, the deployment of digital identity systems, and the government's broader digital transformation agenda. It draws on international frameworks including the GDPR and the African Union's Convention on Cyber Security and Personal Data Protection (the Malabo Convention), as well as the data protection laws of neighboring and peer African nations.

The Proclamation applies to any processing of personal data carried out in Ethiopia, whether by a public or private entity, and to the processing of personal data of persons located in Ethiopia by controllers established outside Ethiopia where such processing relates to offering products or services to those persons or monitoring their behavior in Ethiopia. Processing for exclusively personal or household activities, processing for national security and law enforcement purposes under separate enabling legislation, and processing for journalistic, literary, and artistic purposes are subject to modified requirements or limited exemptions.

A key aim of the Proclamation is to establish Ethiopia as a jurisdiction capable of participating in international data flows with partner countries that have robust data protection requirements, while simultaneously protecting the privacy rights of Ethiopian citizens and facilitating the government's own e-governance and development programs that involve processing personal data at scale.

The **Ethiopian Data Protection Authority (EDPA)** — or the designated national authority with equivalent functions — is established by the Proclamation as the independent supervisory authority responsible for administration, enforcement, and guidance. The EDPA is composed of commissioners appointed by the relevant federal authority and operates with technical independence from line ministries.

### Key Definitions

**Personal data** means any information that relates to an identified or identifiable natural person. A person is identifiable if they can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier such as a name, identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more factors specific to their physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity.

**Sensitive personal data** includes: data revealing racial or ethnic origin; religious beliefs and affiliation; political opinions and party membership; health and medical information; genetic and biometric data; sexual life and orientation; financial information; criminal records; and any other category designated by the EDPA.

**Data controller** (responsable) is any natural or legal person, public authority, or other body that alone or jointly determines the purposes and means of processing personal data.

**Data processor** is any person or body that processes personal data on behalf of and under the instructions of a data controller.

**Data subject** is the natural person to whom the personal data relates.

**Consent** means any freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous indication of the data subject's wishes, expressed through a clear affirmative act, by which the data subject signifies agreement to the processing of personal data relating to them.

**Profiling** means any form of automated processing of personal data used to evaluate, analyze, or predict aspects concerning a natural person's behavior, preferences, economic situation, health, or other personal characteristics.

### Core Obligations for Data Controllers and Processors

**Lawful Processing Grounds.** Processing of personal data must be grounded in one of the following legal bases: consent; contract performance; legal obligation; vital interests of the data subject or another person; public interest; or legitimate interests of the controller or third parties, subject to a proportionality assessment against the data subject's rights.

For sensitive personal data, the Proclamation requires explicit consent, with narrow exceptions for employment and social protection purposes, medical treatment in the public interest, public health, legal proceedings, and non-profit organizations' internal processing of members' data.

**Transparency Obligations.** Before collecting personal data or as soon as practicable thereafter, controllers must provide data subjects with a comprehensive privacy notice. The notice must state the controller's identity and contact details, the purposes and legal bases for processing, the categories of data, intended recipients, international transfers, retention periods, and the data subject's rights.

**Data Quality and Purpose Limitation.** Personal data must be collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes and must not be further processed in a manner incompatible with those purposes. Data must be accurate and, where necessary, kept up to date. Data must be adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary for the processing purposes.

**Security Measures.** Controllers must implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk, including protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction, or damage. Controllers and processors must enter into written data processing agreements specifying the security obligations of the processor.

**Data Breach Notification.** Controllers are required to notify the EDPA of personal data breaches that are likely to result in risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. Notification must be made without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours of becoming aware of the breach. Where the breach is likely to result in high risk, controllers must also notify affected data subjects without undue delay.

**Data Protection Impact Assessments.** Controllers must conduct DPIAs before commencing high-risk processing activities, including large-scale processing of sensitive personal data, systematic monitoring of public areas, processing using new technologies, and profiling that produces significant effects on data subjects. Where a DPIA reveals high residual risk, the controller must consult with the EDPA prior to processing.

**Data Protection Officer.** Certain controllers must appoint a DPO: public authorities processing personal data at large scale; private entities whose core activities consist of regular and systematic monitoring of data subjects at large scale; and entities whose core activities involve large-scale processing of sensitive personal data. The DPO must be registered with the EDPA and have adequate data protection expertise.

**Record-Keeping.** Controllers must maintain detailed records of processing activities documenting purposes, categories of data and data subjects, recipients, transfers, security measures, and retention periods. Records must be made available to the EDPA upon request.

**Children's Data.** Processing of personal data of children requires parental or guardian consent, and controllers must implement age verification measures appropriate to the context. Processing of children's data for advertising or tracking purposes is prohibited without explicit parental consent.

### Rights of Data Subjects

The Proclamation grants data subjects the following rights, exercisable free of charge:

**Right of Access.** The right to obtain confirmation of whether personal data is being processed and, if so, to receive a copy of that data along with information about the processing.

**Right to Correction.** The right to have inaccurate personal data corrected and incomplete personal data completed.

**Right to Erasure.** The right to have personal data deleted where it is no longer necessary, consent has been withdrawn, an objection has been upheld, or processing was unlawful.

**Right to Restriction.** The right to restrict processing while accuracy is contested, an objection is pending, or where the data subject requires the data for legal claims.

**Right to Data Portability.** The right to receive personal data in a structured, commonly used, machine-readable format and to transmit it to another controller, where technically feasible and where processing is based on consent or contract.

**Right to Object.** The right to object to processing based on legitimate interests or for direct marketing and profiling purposes.

**Right to Human Review.** The right not to be subject to solely automated decisions that produce significant effects, including the right to request human review of such decisions.

**Right to Withdraw Consent.** The right to withdraw consent at any time, without affecting the lawfulness of processing before withdrawal.

**Right to Complain.** The right to lodge a complaint with the EDPA.

Controllers must respond to data subject requests within 30 days, extendable by a further 30 days for complex requests. Requests may be refused on limited grounds, including national security, legal obligations, and protection of third parties' rights, but refusals must be notified with reasons.

### Cross-Border Transfer Rules

The Proclamation establishes a framework for international data transfers aimed at protecting personal data when it moves across Ethiopia's borders. Personal data may be transferred to a foreign country only where:

- The EDPA has determined that the recipient country offers an adequate level of protection;
- The controller has put in place appropriate safeguards, such as standard contractual clauses approved by the EDPA, binding corporate rules, or an approved code of conduct;
- A specific derogation applies: the data subject has explicitly consented with full knowledge of the risks; the transfer is necessary for the performance of a contract; the transfer is necessary to protect vital interests; the transfer is for legal claims; or the transfer serves an important public interest recognized under Ethiopian or international law.

Sensitive personal data is subject to stricter transfer restrictions, and certain categories may require EDPA authorization even where an adequacy determination or appropriate safeguards are in place. The EDPA is charged with maintaining and publishing a list of countries assessed as providing adequate protection.

### Automated Processing Provisions

The Proclamation contains dedicated provisions on automated decision-making and profiling, reflecting the growing relevance of AI and algorithmic systems in Ethiopia's digital economy — including in mobile banking, credit scoring, and public service delivery.

Data subjects have the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, that produces legal effects or similarly significantly affects them. Exceptions apply where:

- The automated decision is necessary for entering into or performing a contract with the data subject;
- The decision is authorized by applicable law;
- The data subject has given explicit consent.

Where an automated decision is made pursuant to an exception, the controller must: inform the data subject that the decision was produced by automated means; explain the logic and significance of the processing; allow the data subject to express their view; provide the data subject with the ability to contest the decision; and ensure a qualified human can review and, if appropriate, overturn the automated outcome.

Controllers engaged in profiling must conduct a DPIA and must implement technical and organizational measures to mitigate the risk of discriminatory or erroneous outcomes. The EDPA may issue sector-specific guidance on automated processing, particularly for financial services and public administration.

### Enforcement and Penalties

The EDPA has broad investigatory and enforcement powers, including the ability to conduct inspections, require production of information and documents, issue compliance orders, suspend processing, and impose administrative fines.

The penalty structure under the Proclamation is tiered by severity of violation:

- **Minor violations** (procedural deficiencies, inadequate record-keeping): warnings and fines scaled to the size and resources of the controller.
- **Serious violations** (processing without lawful basis, failing to honor data subject rights, inadequate security measures, unauthorized international transfers): fines of a specified maximum that the EDPA may determine by regulation, taking into account the nature of the violation and the harm caused.
- **Very serious violations** (systematic unlawful processing of sensitive data, processing in violation of explicit prohibition, obstruction of the EDPA): maximum fines and, where appropriate, referral to prosecuting authorities for criminal sanctions.

The Proclamation also provides for civil liability, allowing data subjects to seek compensation for material and non-material harm resulting from violations. In determining penalties, the EDPA must consider the nature, gravity, and duration of the violation; the number of affected data subjects; the severity of harm; the measures taken to mitigate harm; the history of compliance; and the controller's financial capacity.

### Implementation Context

The Ethiopian Personal Data Protection Proclamation arrives at a pivotal moment in the country's digital development. Ethiopia has been rapidly expanding its digital infrastructure, mobile internet penetration is growing, and the government has ambitious e-government and fintech development plans. The Proclamation is intended both to protect Ethiopian citizens' rights in this evolving digital landscape and to create the legal confidence necessary for international technology companies and investors to operate in Ethiopia and for Ethiopia to participate in cross-border digital trade. The EDPA is working to develop implementing regulations and guidance materials in Amharic and other major Ethiopian languages to ensure that the Proclamation's requirements are accessible to entities of all sizes across the country.
