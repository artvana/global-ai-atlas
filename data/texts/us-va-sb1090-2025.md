# High-Risk Artificial Intelligence Developer and Deployer Act (Virginia SB 1090)

## Overview

Virginia Senate Bill 1090, introduced by Senator Saddam Salim in the 2025 legislative session, would have enacted the **High-Risk Artificial Intelligence Developer and Deployer Act** — Virginia's first comprehensive AI governance statute. The bill passed both chambers of the Virginia General Assembly and was enrolled to the Governor. Governor Glenn Youngkin vetoed the bill in April 2025, citing concerns about regulatory burden on technology companies and innovation.

SB 1090 was closely modeled on **Colorado SB 24-205** (the Colorado AI Act, enacted May 2024), applying a risk-based consumer protection framework to AI developers and deployers operating in Virginia. It would have been the second US state comprehensive AI governance law after Colorado.

### Scope

The bill applied to:
- **Developers** of high-risk AI systems that are offered to Virginia consumers or used to make consequential decisions affecting Virginia consumers.
- **Deployers** of high-risk AI systems who use AI to make, or to assist in making, consequential decisions affecting Virginia consumers.
- **High-risk AI system**: an AI system that, when deployed, makes or materially contributes to a consequential decision.
- **Consequential decision**: a decision that has a material legal or similarly significant effect on a consumer regarding education, employment, housing, credit, healthcare, insurance, or legal services.

### Core Obligations

**For Deployers:**
1. Use reasonable care to protect consumers from known or reasonably foreseeable risks of algorithmic discrimination.
2. Complete a documented impact assessment before deployment and annually thereafter, covering training data, intended uses, known limitations, and discrimination mitigation measures.
3. Notify consumers when a high-risk AI system is used to make a consequential decision and explain the basis of the decision.
4. Provide consumers the opportunity to appeal or request human review of consequential decisions.
5. Implement a risk management policy aligned with a recognized framework (e.g., NIST AI RMF).
6. Maintain records of impact assessments for at least three years.

**For Developers:**
1. Make available to deployers documentation about the high-risk AI system including its intended uses, known limitations, performance evaluation data, and data governance measures.
2. Provide summaries of data used to train the system.
3. Cooperate with deployer impact assessments by providing necessary technical information.

### Key Definitions

- **Algorithmic discrimination**: Any condition in which a high-risk AI system contributes to unjustified differential treatment of a consumer based on a protected class (age, color, disability, familial status, gender identity, genetic information, national origin, pregnancy, race, religion, sex, sexual orientation, or veteran status).
- **Impact assessment**: A documented analysis of a high-risk AI system, including its purpose, potential benefits, known limitations, mitigation measures, and review of data governance.

### Penalties and Enforcement

- Enforcement exclusively by the Virginia Attorney General; no private right of action.
- Civil penalties of up to **$10,000 per violation** and up to **$100,000** for willful or knowing violations or patterns of practice.
- AG may seek injunctive relief and restitution.
- A safe harbor for deployers that substantially comply with a recognized national or international AI risk management framework (such as the NIST AI RMF) and have completed a conforming impact assessment.

### Exemptions

- Small businesses (fewer than 50 employees or less than $1 million in annual revenue from Virginia consumers).
- State and local government entities (except for specific high-stakes decisions in criminal justice and public benefits contexts).
- Regulated entities already subject to equivalent federal AI requirements in that sector.
- AI used for research or testing purposes.
- AI systems used solely to detect security threats or prevent fraud.

## Why It Failed

Governor Glenn Youngkin vetoed SB 1090 in April 2025. His veto statement and accompanying materials cited the following concerns:

**1. Regulatory burden on innovation.** Youngkin argued that imposing compliance obligations on AI developers and deployers would disadvantage Virginia-based technology companies relative to competitors in states without such requirements. Virginia has one of the largest concentrations of data centers and technology employers in the US (Northern Virginia / the "Data Center Alley"), making AI policy particularly consequential for the state economy.

**2. Duplicative with federal efforts.** The Governor pointed to federal AI governance initiatives — including the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, ongoing FTC enforcement, and the anticipated federal AI Action Plan — as preferable to state-level regulation that could create a patchwork.

**3. Colorado model problems.** Youngkin noted that Colorado's own legislature and Governor Polis had acknowledged significant problems with SB 24-205 and had been working to amend or delay it since signing (Colorado subsequently delayed the effective date). He argued that Virginia should not replicate a framework its origin state was actively revising.

**4. Vagueness in "high-risk" determinations.** The Governor's office noted that the bill's definitions — particularly "consequential decision" and "algorithmically discriminates" — were broad enough to create significant compliance uncertainty for businesses deploying routine AI tools.

**5. Impact assessment costs.** Small and mid-sized businesses without dedicated AI compliance teams argued the documentation and assessment requirements would be disproportionately burdensome and that the $1 million revenue threshold was too high to provide meaningful small-business relief.

The veto was widely seen as part of a Republican gubernatorial pattern of vetoing state AI consumer protection legislation in 2024–2025, alongside Governor Newsom's veto of SB 1047 in California (though on different grounds) and other rejections. Supporters, including civil rights organizations and consumer advocates, argued the bill provided critical safeguards against discriminatory automated decisions in high-stakes domains.

## Key Provisions (as proposed)

### Algorithmic Impact Assessment Requirements

Deployers of high-risk AI must complete an impact assessment that includes:
- A statement of the purpose and intended uses of the system.
- An overview of the types of data processed by the system.
- An analysis of potential algorithmic discrimination risks.
- Mitigation measures implemented to address identified risks.
- The metrics and performance evaluation methods used.
- Post-deployment monitoring procedures.

### Consumer Rights

Consumers subject to a consequential decision made with a high-risk AI system have the right to:
- Be notified that AI was used in the decision.
- Receive an explanation of the basis for the decision.
- Request human review of the decision.
- Appeal the decision through a meaningful process.
- Receive a written explanation of why the appeal was granted or denied.

### Safe Harbor

A deployer that:
1. Implements a risk management program conforming to the NIST AI RMF or an equivalent nationally or internationally recognized framework, AND
2. Has completed a conforming impact assessment within the prior 12 months

is entitled to a rebuttable presumption that it used reasonable care to prevent algorithmic discrimination.

### Relationship to Colorado SB 205

SB 1090 tracked Colorado's structure closely with these differences:
- Lower civil penalty ceiling ($10,000/$100,000 per violation vs. Colorado's $20,000).
- Slightly narrower "consequential decision" definition.
- Explicit exemption for small businesses by revenue threshold (Colorado exempts by employee count).
- Explicit reference to NIST AI RMF as a safe harbor benchmark (Colorado referred to "nationally recognized" frameworks more generally).

## Policy Significance

Although vetoed, SB 1090 was significant as:
- One of the first state-level comprehensive AI consumer protection bills to pass both chambers of a legislature in 2025.
- Confirmation that the Colorado SB 205 model was politically viable enough to advance in other state legislatures, even if ultimate enactment remained difficult.
- Part of a wave of 2025 state AI bills modeled on Colorado's risk-based framework, including bills in Texas (HB 149 / TRAIGA), Washington (HB 2225), and others.
- A data point in the debate over whether Republican-led states would pass any comprehensive AI consumer protection legislation.
