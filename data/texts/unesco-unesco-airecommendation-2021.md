# UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence (SHS/BIO/REC-AIETHICS/2021)

## Overview

The Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence was adopted by the General Conference of UNESCO at its 41st session held in Paris from 9 to 24 November 2021. It is the first-ever global standard on AI ethics applicable to all 194 UNESCO member states. The Recommendation was prepared by the Ad Hoc Expert Group (AHEG) convened by UNESCO.

The protection of human rights and dignity is the cornerstone of the Recommendation, based on the advancement of fundamental principles such as transparency and fairness, always remembering the importance of human oversight of AI systems. The Recommendation pays special attention to the ethical implications of AI systems in relation to culture, education, science, information and communication, and aims to guide policymakers and stakeholders on how to ensure that AI is developed and used ethically.

The Recommendation interprets AI broadly as systems with the ability to process data in a way which resembles intelligent behaviour. This is crucial as the rapid pace of technological change would quickly render any fixed, narrow definition outdated, and make future-proof policies infeasible.

---

## PART I: SCOPE OF APPLICATION

The Recommendation applies to all stages of the AI system lifecycle, including research, design, development, deployment, and use of AI systems. It addresses all actors involved: governments, intergovernmental organizations, the private sector, civil society, research institutions, academic institutions, media organizations, and educational institutions.

---

## PART II: AIMS AND OBJECTIVES

The aims of this Recommendation are to:

- Provide a universal framework of values, principles and actions to guide States in formulating their legislation, policies or other instruments regarding AI
- Guide individuals and groups acting at any point in the AI system lifecycle
- Encourage interdisciplinary and international research on the ethical challenges posed by AI systems
- Promote collaborative approaches to develop ethical AI governance at the national, regional and international levels
- Protect and promote human rights and fundamental freedoms and human dignity throughout the lifecycle of AI systems
- Ensure the protection of the environment and ecosystems
- Prevent and mitigate the risks of AI while maximizing its benefits to improve human wellbeing

---

## PART III: DEFINITIONS

**Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems** refer to information processing technologies that incorporate models and algorithms that produce a capacity to learn and to perform cognitive tasks leading to outcomes such as prediction, recommendations, decisions, or the generation of content, which influence real and virtual environments. AI systems are designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy.

**AI lifecycle** refers to the stages of development and deployment of an AI system including (1) research, design and development; (2) deployment and use; and (3) discontinuation. This includes phases of data collection and procurement, pre-processing, building of models, testing and evaluation, deployment in real world contexts, and monitoring and evaluation of deployed systems.

**AI actors** refers to those who research, design, develop, deploy, or use AI systems, including governments, intergovernmental organizations, the private sector, civil society organizations and groups, research institutions and academic institutions, media organizations and journalists, and educational institutions and teachers.

**Ethics of AI** refers to the system of values and moral principles that should guide the development, deployment, and use of AI systems.

---

## PART IV: CORE VALUES

Central to the Recommendation are four core values which lay the foundations for AI systems that work for the good of humanity, individuals, societies and the environment:

### Value 1: Human Rights and Human Dignity

Respect, protection and promotion of human rights and fundamental freedoms and human dignity. AI actors should ensure that AI systems do not adversely affect the enjoyment of human rights and fundamental freedoms. AI governance should be grounded in a commitment to these universal and inalienable rights, including civil and political rights, economic, social and cultural rights, and the right to development. In particular, AI actors should pay due regard to the right to privacy, the right to non-discrimination, and the right to equality before the law.

AI systems should be developed in the interest of humanity at large, inclusive of future generations, and in accordance with the goals of peace, justice, democracy, and environmental wellbeing, as well as the realization of the full spectrum of human rights. AI systems should respect human dignity and the autonomy of individuals, and should support the capacity of humans to make free, informed choices.

### Value 2: Living in Peaceful, Just, and Interconnected Societies

AI should not be used in a way that undermines peaceful, just, and interconnected societies. The commitment to this value means recognizing that peaceful coexistence among different peoples, cultures, and nations is a fundamental goal. AI systems should promote justice, inclusion, solidarity, and democratic participation. AI actors should work to prevent the misuse of AI to spread hate speech, propaganda, or disinformation that could be harmful to societies.

This value emphasizes that democratic institutions, the rule of law, and equitable governance depend on AI systems that promote rather than undermine civic trust, democratic deliberation, and rule-based international order. It also encompasses the right to development and global solidarity, including ensuring that less developed nations can benefit equitably from AI technologies.

### Value 3: Ensuring Diversity and Inclusiveness

AI systems should be designed and used in a manner that ensures diversity and inclusiveness, recognizing and preserving cultural diversity, and working towards genuine cultural plurality. This value requires attention to the needs and perspectives of all groups—including those defined by age, culture, language, physical or cognitive ability, gender, socioeconomic status, and geographic location.

Member States should cultivate an environment that encourages unimpeded entry into AI systems that provide locally relevant content and services, while maintaining respect for multilingualism and cultural plurality, which extends to both local and foreign actors. UNESCO has called on technologically advanced nations to maintain a global obligation of solidarity with less developed nations to ensure equitable distribution of the benefits of AI, thereby facilitating participation and access to information systems. This effort serves to promote a more equitable world order with respect to information, education, culture, research, socioeconomic conditions, and even political stability.

### Value 4: Environment and Ecosystem Flourishing

The development and use of AI systems must be assessed in view of their environmental and ecosystem impacts. Environmental sustainability, ecosystem flourishing, and respect for planetary boundaries must be considered throughout the AI system lifecycle. AI actors should work to ensure that AI systems contribute to mitigating and adapting to climate change, to biodiversity conservation, and to sustainable development.

## PART V: PRINCIPLES

Ten core principles lay out a human-rights centred approach to the Ethics of AI. These principles are interrelated and should be read in conjunction with each other:

### Principle 1: Proportionality and Do No Harm

The use of AI systems must not go beyond what is necessary to achieve a legitimate aim. Risk assessment should be used to prevent harms which may result from such uses. AI actors should implement measures to ensure that the potential benefits of AI systems are proportional to the risks posed to individuals and society. AI systems should not be used for purposes that go beyond their stated objectives or that could cause unnecessary harm.

According to this principle, the application of AI must remain within limits to achieve the predetermined objective, without venturing into excessive or unnecessary utilization that exceeds the established objective. For example, if AI is used to detect and prevent crimes, it must be ensured that the measures taken are proportional to the risk of committing it and not use too much force or resources on a target beyond what is established. AI systems must not violate the fundamental principles of human rights and must be well adapted to the specific circumstances that arise, based on reliable scientific principles.

Safety refers to the need to avoid the use of AI for purposes that may cause harm or harm to people or the environment. AI should not be used to discriminate against certain groups, for mass surveillance or for psychological manipulation. The chosen AI method must be appropriate and balanced to achieve a specific legitimate objective.

### Principle 2: Safety and Security

Unwanted harms (safety risks) as well as vulnerabilities to attack (security risks) should be avoided and addressed by AI actors throughout the lifecycle of AI systems. To ensure the safety of humans, the environment and ecosystems, it is crucial to address and mitigate unwanted harms, security risks and vulnerabilities that can lead to attacks throughout the entire systems lifecycle of AI. This involves taking proactive measures to prevent and eliminate such risks.

To effectively promote AI safety, it is essential to establish sustainable frameworks for accessing data. These frameworks should prioritize privacy while allowing for improved training and validation of AI models using high-quality data. In this way, a solid foundation can be laid for AI systems that prioritize the well-being of people and the preservation of the natural environment.

AI actors must also work to ensure the integrity of AI systems themselves against adversarial attacks, interference, and manipulation. Security measures should encompass not only the AI systems but also the data and infrastructure on which they rely.

### Principle 3: Right to Privacy and Data Protection

Privacy must be protected and promoted throughout the AI lifecycle. Adequate data protection frameworks should also be established. Privacy must be respected, safeguarded and promoted because it is a fundamental human right and, therefore, defended as part of human dignity. The collection, use, sharing, archiving and deletion of data must be carried out in accordance with applicable legal frameworks.

In order to establish global data protection standards and governance procedures, supported by legal systems, a multilateral approach is recommended. The principles and regulations governing the harvesting, use and dissemination of personal data, together with the exercise of rights by data subjects, must be assimilated within local data protection laws and any accompanying mechanisms. These frameworks must also ensure the presence of a valid objective and legal justification for the processing of personal data, which includes obtaining informed consent.

Privacy is the fact that personal data is not disclosed. Personal data is any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person, including direct information such as name and surname, physical address and email address, telephone number, date of birth, gender, nationality, identification number, financial information, credit card or bank account details, medical information, employment, education, IP address, and geolocation.

AI actors should take a privacy-by-design approach, embedding privacy considerations throughout the AI system lifecycle from the earliest stages of design. AI systems should minimize data collection to what is strictly necessary, implement appropriate data security measures, and ensure that individuals can exercise their rights in relation to their personal data.

### Principle 4: Multi-Stakeholder and Adaptive Governance and Collaboration

International law and national sovereignty must be respected in the use of data. Additionally, participation of diverse stakeholders is necessary for inclusive approaches to AI governance. Large multinational companies that use massive amounts of information must respect both national sovereignty and international law. Countries are free to regulate data created in or passing through their territory, and to adopt measures for effective oversight of the use of their citizens' information, including protection and respect for the right to privacy, and other standards of fundamental right.

To ensure the equitable distribution of benefits and the promotion of sustainable development in AI, it is crucial to have the participation of all relevant entities, including governments, non-governmental organizations, civil society, academia, media, educators and decision makers in the public and private sectors. Collaboration between the parties involved is necessary to facilitate the adoption of open and interoperable standards.

UNESCO recommends taking measures to take into account technological changes, the creation of new groups and the significant participation of marginalized communities and individuals, as well as respect for the self-management of their data. AI governance mechanisms should be adaptive and capable of evolving as technologies change.

### Principle 5: Responsibility and Accountability

AI systems should be auditable and traceable. There should be oversight, impact assessment, audit and due diligence mechanisms in place to avoid conflicts with human rights norms and threats to environmental wellbeing. AI actors must always assume their ethical duty and responsibility for decisions and actions that are based in any way on an AI system.

UNESCO emphasizes that responsibility must be in accordance with the international and national legislation of each country, in particular in matters of human rights. To guarantee responsibility, appropriate supervision, impact and evaluation processes must be designed through audits of each process. This multifaceted approach seeks to strengthen the ethical framework for accountability and promotes a culture of transparency, trust and responsible implementation of AI.

The allocation of responsibility along the AI value chain must be clearly established. Where AI systems cause harm, there must be effective mechanisms for redress and remedy. AI actors should also take steps to ensure that third parties to whom they delegate AI-related tasks also meet appropriate ethical standards.

### Principle 6: Transparency and Explainability

The ethical deployment of AI systems depends on their transparency and explainability. The level of transparency and explainability (T&E) should be appropriate to the context, as there may be tensions between T&E and other principles such as privacy, safety and security. Transparency and explainability are fundamental prerequisites to ensure respect, protection and promotion of human rights, fundamental freedoms and ethical principles.

Transparency is necessary for the effective functioning of national and international liability regulations, where they exist, and to facilitate the review of decisions made by AI and thus strengthen the legal areas in which these systems can be used. The degree of transparency and explainability must be proportional to the context and the effect in any democratic regime.

The public must be properly informed when a decision comes from AI algorithms, especially if it affects security or human rights. Therefore, the public should have the opportunity to seek clarification and information from the responsible AI actor or relevant government entities in these circumstances. Subjects of AI must be able to understand the basis of any decision that concerns them, as well as have the option to complain to a qualified member of staff of a private sector company or public institution, for review and, if necessary, any result is modified if it is unfairly adverse.

The goal of transparency is to give adequate information to each recipient so that they can understand and develop trust in the system and, ultimately, in their own government or company that provides them with a service. Transparency can help people understand how each stage is carried out and reveals information if appropriate safeguards, such as security measures or impartiality, have been put in place.

Explainability is the ability to make the results of AI systems understandable. This also includes understanding the input, output, operation, and contribution of each algorithmic building element to the final product. Results and subprocesses must also be understandable and traceable.

### Principle 7: Human Oversight and Determination

Member States should ensure that AI systems do not displace ultimate human responsibility and accountability. Human supervision refers to both individual and collective control, if necessary. The decision to hand over control to AI in certain situations can be made by people for efficiency reasons, but this will only be the case in limited circumstances.

Although humans can use AI systems to help them make decisions and perform tasks, the system will not be able to replace the ultimate responsibility of the operator or whoever gives the order, and their obligation to be accountable for their actions. Therefore, there are decisions that should not be left in the hands of machines; for example, decisions that put the life or health of another person at risk.

This principle refers to the attribution of responsibility, that is, the ability to identify who is responsible for the decisions of AI systems. Decisions are made automatically by algorithms and mathematical models based on data. Therefore, it can be difficult to identify who is responsible for AI decisions. Human supervision is required to ensure that such decisions remain traceable and that accountable humans bear ultimate responsibility.

### Principle 8: Sustainability

AI technologies should be assessed against their impacts on sustainability, understood as a set of constantly evolving goals including those set out in the UN's Sustainable Development Goals. For the development of sustainable societies it is necessary to advance not only environmental dimensions, but also social, cultural and economic dimensions. Depending on how AI technologies are applied in nations with different degrees of development, they can help or hinder the achievement of sustainability goals.

Energy efficiency means that AI systems must be designed to minimize consumption and, therefore, reduce their carbon footprint. Life cycle considerations require assessing the environmental and social impacts of AI systems during and after their useful life, from production to disposal. Interoperability means that AI systems must be compatible with other existing systems and technologies, reducing the need to create new systems and reducing duplication of efforts.

AI systems should be designed and operated in ways that minimize their environmental impact and contribute positively to ecological balance. This includes minimizing energy use, reducing electronic waste, and ensuring that AI applications support rather than undermine sustainable development.

### Principle 9: Awareness and Literacy

Public understanding of AI and data should be promoted through open and accessible education, civic engagement, digital skills and AI ethics training, media and information literacy. To ensure the effectiveness of public policies, it is imperative that governments, non-governmental organizations, academia, the media, community leaders, civil associations and the private sector collaborate in promoting media literacy, civic engagement and training in digital skills. These efforts must take into account existing cultural and linguistic diversity.

Additionally, a concerted effort must be made to cultivate a culture of ethical AI across all sectors, including academia, research, government, and industry. To do this, AI systems must be designed taking into account responsible practical considerations, such as transparency, accountability and respect for human rights. Mechanisms for continuous monitoring and evaluation of AI systems must be established to ensure their compliance with ethical norms and standards.

This principle applies to all stakeholders in the AI ecosystem, from developers and deployers to end users and those affected by AI decisions. Building widespread AI literacy is essential to enabling meaningful participation in the governance of AI and in exercising rights in relation to AI decisions.

### Principle 10: Fairness and Non-Discrimination

AI actors should promote social justice, fairness, and non-discrimination while taking an inclusive approach to ensure AI's benefits are accessible to all. AI entities are required to safeguard the principles of equity, social justice and non-discrimination. This mandate presents an integrative perspective to ensure equitable availability and accessibility of the benefits of AI technology for all, taking into account the different needs of disparate demographic groups, such as those defined by age, culture, language, physical or cognitive ability, gender and socioeconomic status.

Member States should cultivate an environment that encourages unimpeded entry into AI systems that provide locally relevant content and services, while maintaining respect for multilingualism and cultural plurality, which extends to both local and foreign actors. This effort aims to bridge the digital divide and encourage equitable access and participation in the evolution of AI. At national level, Member States should strive for equity in access and participation throughout the lifecycle of AI systems, between rural and urban areas, and people of all genders, ages, religions, races, political affiliations, ethnicities, socioeconomic positions, disabilities or any other reason.

UNESCO has called on technologically advanced nations to maintain a global obligation of solidarity with less developed nations to ensure equitable distribution of the benefits of AI, thereby facilitating participation and access to information systems.

## PART VI: POLICY ACTION AREAS

The Recommendation sets out eleven key areas for policy action to translate values and principles into concrete measures. These are:

### Policy Area 1: Ethical Impact Assessment

Member States, in consultation with multidisciplinary and diverse stakeholders, should develop or adapt existing assessment methodologies and tools, such as ethical impact assessments, to evaluate the impact of AI systems on individuals, on society and on the environment and ecosystems. Such assessments should consider the full lifecycle of AI systems, from data collection to system design, development, deployment, and decommissioning.

Ethical impact assessments should identify potential harms and benefits and provide guidance on risk mitigation. They should be made publicly available where appropriate, and regularly updated as AI systems evolve. Organizations and individuals deploying AI systems should be required to conduct ethical impact assessments proportionate to the potential risks of the systems they deploy.

### Policy Area 2: Ethical Governance and Stewardship

Member States should develop and enforce relevant legal and regulatory frameworks for the ethical development, deployment and use of AI systems, in a manner consistent with international human rights law and the provisions of this Recommendation. This includes ensuring that AI actors operating in their territory are subject to meaningful oversight and accountability mechanisms.

Governance frameworks should be adaptive and capable of responding to rapidly changing AI capabilities. Multi-stakeholder governance bodies that include civil society, technical experts, ethicists, legal experts, and affected communities should be established. Governance arrangements should promote international cooperation and harmonization while respecting national sovereignty.

### Policy Area 3: Data Policy

Member States should develop appropriate legal and regulatory frameworks for data governance, including data protection laws, to ensure that AI systems use data in a manner that is ethical, rights-respecting, and in compliance with applicable legal frameworks. Data policies should address data collection, processing, storage, sharing, use and deletion throughout the AI system lifecycle.

Particular attention should be paid to protecting sensitive data categories, including health data, biometric data, and data related to children. Data governance frameworks should promote data quality, representativeness, and diversity to reduce bias. International data sharing should be governed by agreements that protect the interests of individuals and respect national data sovereignty.

### Policy Area 4: Development and International Cooperation

Member States should seek to promote international cooperation on the development and sharing of AI technologies, knowledge, and capacity for the common good, particularly in support of less technologically advanced countries. This includes supporting capacity-building initiatives, technology transfer arrangements, and inclusive governance processes that allow all countries to participate in shaping the global AI landscape.

Development-oriented AI applications should be prioritized and supported, including AI systems that contribute to achieving the Sustainable Development Goals. International development assistance should include AI-related capacity building. South-South and triangular cooperation in AI development and governance should be promoted.

### Policy Area 5: Environment and Ecosystems

Member States should ensure that the development and use of AI systems are environmentally sustainable. Policies should be developed to assess and mitigate the environmental impact of AI systems throughout their lifecycle, including energy consumption, water use, electronic waste, and other environmental impacts.

AI should be leveraged to support environmental protection and sustainability, including climate change mitigation and adaptation, biodiversity conservation, and sustainable natural resource management. Environmental considerations should be integrated into AI system design, development, and deployment. Developers and deployers of AI systems should be required to disclose and minimize the environmental footprint of their systems.

### Policy Area 6: Gender

Member States should take specific measures to address gender biases in AI systems and to promote gender equality in the AI ecosystem. This includes promoting the participation of women and girls in AI education, research, and development; addressing algorithmic bias that perpetuates gender stereotypes; and ensuring that AI systems do not exacerbate existing gender inequalities.

Policies should ensure that AI systems are designed and tested to avoid discrimination on the basis of gender, gender identity, and sexual orientation. Gender-disaggregated data collection and analysis should be promoted to better understand the differential impacts of AI on different genders. AI systems used in high-stakes domains such as employment, healthcare, and justice should be subject to gender audits.

### Policy Area 7: Culture

Member States should work to protect and promote cultural diversity in the context of AI development and deployment. This includes supporting the development of AI systems that respect and represent diverse languages, cultures, traditions, and knowledge systems. Policies should prevent AI systems from marginalizing or erasing minority cultures and languages.

Cultural heritage institutions should be supported in using AI ethically to preserve and promote cultural heritage. Intellectual property frameworks should be adapted to address the challenges posed by AI-generated content and AI's use of cultural materials. Cultural considerations should be integrated into AI ethics education and training.

### Policy Area 8: Education and Research

Member States should integrate AI ethics into education at all levels, from primary and secondary education through to higher education and continuing professional development. AI literacy programmes should be developed and made available to all citizens. Researchers and educators should have access to resources and support to develop and teach AI ethics.

Research on the ethics, social impacts, and governance of AI should be supported and funded. Interdisciplinary research that brings together technical, social, legal, and ethical perspectives on AI should be encouraged. Research findings should be made openly available where possible to support evidence-based AI governance.

Member States should work to reduce the global disparities in AI research capacity, including by supporting AI research institutions in developing countries and promoting international research collaboration.

### Policy Area 9: Communication and Information

Member States should promote AI literacy and critical thinking skills among media professionals, journalists, and citizens to enable them to identify and respond to AI-generated content, including deepfakes, synthetic media, and automated content generation. Media and information literacy education should address the ethical implications of AI in the production and distribution of information.

Policies should address the use of AI in content recommendation and curation, ensuring that algorithmic systems do not exacerbate polarization, misinformation, or the undermining of democratic discourse. Transparency requirements should apply to AI systems used in media, news, and information contexts.

### Policy Area 10: Work and Labour Market

Member States should develop policies to manage the impact of AI on work and the labour market, including by supporting workers through reskilling and upskilling programmes, strengthening social protection systems, and engaging in social dialogue about the future of work in the age of AI.

AI systems used in employment contexts—including for recruitment, performance management, task assignment, and monitoring—should be subject to appropriate safeguards to protect workers' rights and dignity. Workers should have the right to information about AI systems used in their workplaces and the right to challenge decisions made on the basis of such systems.

Policies should aim to ensure that the productivity gains from AI are broadly shared and contribute to improved working conditions and quality of life rather than simply to the enrichment of a small number of stakeholders.

### Policy Area 11: Health and Social Wellbeing

Member States should ensure that AI systems used in health and social care contexts are subject to appropriate ethical safeguards and regulatory oversight. AI systems used for medical diagnosis, treatment recommendation, or care management should be validated for safety and efficacy before deployment and monitored on an ongoing basis.

Patient rights—including the right to informed consent, the right to non-discrimination, and the right to human review of AI-assisted decisions affecting their care—should be protected. Health data should be subject to strong privacy protections and appropriate governance frameworks.

AI should be leveraged to improve health equity, including by improving access to care in underserved communities and reducing disparities in health outcomes. Social care AI applications should be designed and deployed in ways that enhance, rather than replace, human connection and care.

---

## PART VII: MONITORING AND REVIEW

Member States should establish appropriate national mechanisms to monitor and review the implementation of this Recommendation. UNESCO should collect information on steps taken by Member States to implement the Recommendation and report on progress to its General Conference.

Regular review of the Recommendation itself should be conducted to ensure that it remains appropriate in the light of technological developments and evolving ethical understanding. Multi-stakeholder consultation should be incorporated into all monitoring and review processes.

---

## Implementation Framework

UNESCO has developed two practical methodologies to support implementation:

**Readiness Assessment Methodology (RAM):** The RAM is designed to help assess whether Member States are prepared to effectively implement the Recommendation. It will help them identify their status of preparedness and provide a basis for UNESCO to custom-tailor its capacity-building support.

**Ethical Impact Assessment (EIA):** The EIA is a structured process which helps AI project teams, in collaboration with the affected communities, to identify and assess the impacts an AI system may have. It allows reflection on its potential impact and to identify needed harm prevention actions.
