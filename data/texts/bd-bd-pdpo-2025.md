## Summary and Analysis

### Purpose and Scope

The Bangladesh Personal Data Protection Ordinance 2025 is Bangladesh's first comprehensive, standalone statute governing the collection, processing, storage, and transfer of personal data. Promulgated as an Ordinance in 2025 — reflecting the legislative mechanism available under the interim government that followed the political transition of 2024 — the Ordinance draws heavily on the GDPR model and on the data protection frameworks of neighboring countries, including India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023. Its primary purpose is to protect the fundamental right to privacy of natural persons in Bangladesh while enabling the lawful use of personal data for legitimate commercial, governmental, and social purposes.

The Ordinance applies to the processing of personal data where: the data fiduciary (controller) or data processor is established in Bangladesh; the processing relates to offering goods or services to data principals (data subjects) located in Bangladesh; or the processing involves the monitoring of behavior of data principals in Bangladesh insofar as that behavior takes place within Bangladesh. The Ordinance covers both automated and manual processing of personal data held in structured systems. It applies to private entities and, to a significant extent, to government bodies processing personal data of citizens, though certain national security and law enforcement processing is carved out.

The Ordinance establishes the Bangladesh Data Protection Authority (BDPA) as the regulatory body responsible for implementation and enforcement. The BDPA operates under the Ministry of Posts, Telecommunications and Information Technology and is charged with issuing guidance, codes of practice, and binding regulations; receiving and adjudicating complaints; conducting audits; and imposing penalties.

### Key Definitions

**Personal data** means any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person. An identifiable person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, by reference to an identifier such as a name, identification number, location data, an online identifier, or one or more factors specific to that person's physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity.

**Sensitive personal data** is given a broad definition encompassing: data about race, ethnicity, or national origin; political opinions; religious or philosophical beliefs; trade union membership; genetic data; biometric data used for unique identification; health data; sex life or sexual orientation; financial information including bank account details; and data relating to criminal records or past offenses.

**Data fiduciary** (equivalent to a controller under GDPR) means any person, company, firm, state instrumentality, or body corporate that alone or in conjunction with others determines the purpose and means of processing personal data.

**Data processor** means any person or body that processes personal data on behalf of and under the instruction of a data fiduciary.

**Data principal** means the natural person to whom the personal data relates.

**Consent** means a free, specific, informed, unconditional, and unambiguous indication of the data principal's wishes by which, by a clear affirmative act, the data principal signifies agreement to the processing of personal data for a specified purpose.

**Significant data fiduciary** is a category of data fiduciary that the BDPA may designate based on the volume and sensitivity of data processed, the risk to data principals, potential impact on national security, and the relevance of the processing to public order. Significant data fiduciaries are subject to additional obligations including mandatory data protection impact assessments, appointment of a Data Protection Officer, and stricter cross-border transfer controls.

### Core Obligations for Data Fiduciaries and Processors

**Lawful Processing Grounds.** Personal data may be processed only where: the data principal has given consent; processing is necessary for the performance of a contract with the data principal; processing is required for compliance with a legal obligation of the data fiduciary; processing is necessary to protect the vital interests of the data principal or another person; processing is carried out for public interest functions; or processing serves the legitimate interests of the data fiduciary, provided those interests are not overridden by the rights and interests of the data principal.

**Purpose Limitation and Data Minimization.** Data fiduciaries must collect personal data only for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes and must not process it in a manner incompatible with those purposes. The data collected must be limited to what is necessary for the stated purpose.

**Accuracy and Storage Limitation.** Data fiduciaries must take reasonable steps to ensure that personal data is accurate, complete, and updated as necessary for the purposes of processing, and must not retain personal data for longer than is necessary for such purposes.

**Security Safeguards.** Data fiduciaries are required to implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to protect personal data against loss, unauthorized access, modification, or disclosure. The Ordinance prescribes that security measures must be proportionate to the nature of the data and the likelihood and severity of risk.

**Data Breach Notification.** A distinctive and prescriptive feature of the Bangladesh Ordinance is its **90-day breach notification requirement**. Upon becoming aware of a personal data breach, a data fiduciary must notify the BDPA within 90 days of becoming aware of the breach if the breach is likely to result in harm to data principals. In cases of high-risk breaches, the data fiduciary must also notify affected data principals within a reasonable period. The 90-day period is notably longer than the 72-hour standard under GDPR and reflects a pragmatic adjustment for resource constraints in Bangladesh's business environment, particularly for smaller entities.

**Data Protection Impact Assessments.** Where processing is likely to result in high risk to the rights of data principals — including large-scale processing of sensitive personal data, systematic profiling, processing involving new technologies, or processing of children's data — data fiduciaries must conduct a DPIA before commencing the processing. Significant data fiduciaries are required to conduct DPIAs for all high-risk processing activities.

**Data Protection Officer (DPO).** Significant data fiduciaries must appoint a Data Protection Officer who reports directly to the highest level of management. The DPO must be a qualified professional with knowledge of data protection law and practice. DPO functions include advising on compliance, monitoring adherence to the Ordinance, cooperating with the BDPA, and serving as a point of contact for data principals. Non-significant data fiduciaries may appoint a DPO voluntarily, and the Ordinance encourages this practice for entities processing significant volumes of personal data.

**Children's Data.** The Ordinance includes specific protections for the personal data of children under 18, requiring verifiable parental or guardian consent before processing a child's data for most purposes. Processing of children's data for advertising or tracking purposes is prohibited without explicit consent. Data fiduciaries must implement age verification mechanisms appropriate to the context.

**Processor Agreements.** Data fiduciaries engaging processors must do so under a binding written agreement that sets out the scope, nature, and purposes of processing and mandates compliance with the Ordinance. Sub-processors may only be engaged with the prior authorization of the data fiduciary, and processors must notify data fiduciaries of any actual or suspected personal data breach.

### Rights of Data Principals

The Bangladesh Ordinance enumerates **14 data subject rights**, making it one of the most comprehensive in the region:

1. **Right to information** about the processing of personal data before or at the time of collection.
2. **Right of access** to personal data being processed and to obtain a copy.
3. **Right to rectification** of inaccurate or incomplete data.
4. **Right to erasure** (the "right to be forgotten") where data is no longer necessary, consent is withdrawn, or processing is unlawful.
5. **Right to restriction of processing** while accuracy is contested or an objection is pending.
6. **Right to data portability** to receive data in a structured, machine-readable format and to have it transmitted to another fiduciary.
7. **Right to object** to processing based on legitimate interests, including profiling for marketing.
8. **Rights in relation to automated decision-making** — not to be subject to solely automated decisions that produce significant legal or similarly significant effects, and to request human review.
9. **Right to withdraw consent** at any time without detriment.
10. **Right to be informed of data breaches** that are likely to result in high risk.
11. **Right to complain** to the BDPA.
12. **Right to seek compensation** for material and non-material damage from violations.
13. **Right to nominate** a representative to exercise rights on one's behalf (including posthumous data rights protection).
14. **Right to grievance redressal** through the data fiduciary's internal mechanism before escalating to the BDPA.

Data fiduciaries must respond to data principal requests within 30 days. Requests may be refused on limited grounds, including national security, prevention of crime, and protection of the rights of others, but refusals must be notified to the data principal along with reasons.

### Cross-Border Transfer Rules

The Ordinance establishes a restrictive approach to international data transfers. Personal data may be transferred to a country outside Bangladesh only where:

- The BDPA has determined that the recipient country provides an adequate level of data protection;
- The transfer is subject to appropriate safeguards such as standard contractual clauses, binding corporate rules, or an approved code of conduct; or
- A specific derogation applies, such as the explicit consent of the data principal, the necessity of the transfer for contract performance, or vital interests.

**Sensitive personal data** and data processed by significant data fiduciaries is subject to stricter transfer controls. Certain categories of sensitive data may be subject to a **data localization requirement**, meaning copies must be retained on servers located in Bangladesh. The BDPA is empowered to issue regulations specifying categories of data that are subject to localization, and the Ordinance anticipates that the BDPA will publish a list of approved jurisdictions for data transfers.

### Automated Decision-Making Provisions

The Ordinance dedicates specific provisions to automated decision-making and profiling, reflecting the global trend toward explicit regulation of AI-driven decisions. Data principals have the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing — including profiling — that produces legal effects or similarly significant effects on them, such as decisions about employment, creditworthiness, access to public services, insurance pricing, or housing.

Exceptions apply where: the decision is necessary for entering into or performing a contract with the data principal; the decision is required by law; or the data principal has given explicit consent. Even where an exception applies, the data fiduciary must: inform the data principal that the decision was made by automated means; explain the logic, significance, and consequences of the automated processing; provide the data principal with the right to request human review of the decision; enable the data principal to express their point of view; and provide the data principal with the ability to contest the decision.

Data fiduciaries using automated systems for profiling must conduct a DPIA and must implement measures to prevent discriminatory outcomes based on race, gender, religion, or other protected characteristics.

### Enforcement and Penalties

The BDPA has broad investigatory and enforcement powers, including the ability to conduct inspections, require production of records and information, issue compliance orders, and impose administrative penalties. The Ordinance also establishes a Data Protection Appellate Tribunal to hear appeals against BDPA decisions.

The maximum administrative fine under the Ordinance is **BDT 5 crore** (approximately USD 450,000 at 2025 exchange rates), representing a significant penalty in the Bangladesh context. The penalty structure is tiered:

- Less serious violations (minor procedural failures, record-keeping deficiencies): fines up to BDT 50 lakh.
- Moderate violations (failure to obtain consent, inadequate security measures, failure to notify breach): fines up to BDT 2 crore.
- Serious violations (unlawful processing of sensitive data, unauthorized international transfers, obstruction of the BDPA): fines up to BDT 5 crore.

The Ordinance also provides for criminal liability for the most serious violations, including intentional and unauthorized disclosure of sensitive personal data for gain or malicious purposes, with penalties including imprisonment of up to three years, fines, or both.

In determining penalties, the BDPA must consider: the nature, gravity, and duration of the violation; the number of affected data principals; the measures taken to mitigate damage; the history of compliance; and the financial capacity of the data fiduciary.

### Implementation and Transition

Given that the Ordinance was promulgated in 2025, the BDPA has been granted a phased implementation timeline. Large enterprises and significant data fiduciaries were required to achieve full compliance within 12 months of the Ordinance's commencement. Medium-sized enterprises have an 18-month window, and small and micro enterprises benefit from a 24-month transition period. The BDPA has been directed to issue sector-specific guidance and codes of practice to assist entities in compliance, with particular focus on the financial services, healthcare, and telecommunications sectors.
