## Utah Senate Bill 226 (2025)
### Artificial Intelligence Consumer Protection Amendments

**2025 Utah General Legislative Session**
**Enacted:** March 13, 2025 | **Effective:** May 7, 2025

---

## Overview

Utah Senate Bill 226 (2025) amends and strengthens Utah's groundbreaking 2024 Artificial Intelligence Policy Act (SB 149, Utah Code § 13-2-8), which was the first US state law specifically regulating AI in consumer-facing interactions. SB 226 tightens disclosure requirements for regulated occupations using generative AI, introduces a "clear and conspicuous" statutory safe harbor standard, expands the definition of high-risk AI interactions to cover additional sensitive data categories, and adds several compliance clarifications for regulated professionals.

SB 226 is a refinement bill — building on the framework established by SB 149 in 2024 rather than creating new regulatory architecture — and reflects Utah's approach of iterative, practical AI governance.

---

## Legislative Background

Utah's 2024 AI Policy Act (SB 149) required that persons regulated by certain professional licensing bodies disclose when they are using generative AI in interactions with consumers, particularly in contexts involving sensitive information. In the year since SB 149's enactment, the Utah Division of Consumer Protection and regulated industry groups identified several ambiguities in the original law:

1. The standard for "adequate" disclosure was unclear, creating inconsistent compliance practices;
2. Certain categories of sensitive AI interactions were not explicitly covered;
3. The safe harbor for good-faith disclosure needed codification;
4. Small-practice licensed professionals needed clearer guidance on their obligations.

SB 226 addresses each of these issues.

## Amendments to Utah Code § 13-2-8

### Section 1 — Enhanced Disclosure Standard ("Clear and Conspicuous")

SB 226 amends the AI disclosure requirement in Utah Code § 13-2-8 to replace the general "disclosure" requirement with a "clear and conspicuous disclosure" standard. A disclosure is clear and conspicuous if it:

1. Is presented at the outset of the interaction and in a manner reasonably designed to be noticed and understood by the consumer;
2. States, in plain language, that the interaction involves the use of generative artificial intelligence;
3. Identifies the general nature of the AI's role (e.g., drafting assistance, direct consumer interaction, decision support);
4. Is not buried in terms of service, fine print, or other disclosures unlikely to be read by consumers.

The "clear and conspicuous" standard aligns Utah's AI disclosure requirement with existing FTC advertising disclosure standards, providing regulated professionals familiar guidance.

### Section 2 — Statutory Safe Harbor

A regulated person who provides a clear and conspicuous disclosure pursuant to Section 1 is entitled to a statutory safe harbor from liability under this Act for:

1. Alleged failures to disclose AI use, where the disclosure satisfies the clear and conspicuous standard;
2. Consumer protection claims arising from AI use, where the regulated person has complied with disclosure requirements and has not used AI in a manner that independently violates applicable professional standards.

The safe harbor does not protect against:
- Violations of professional licensing standards arising from the AI use itself (e.g., malpractice, breach of fiduciary duty);
- Fraud or intentional misrepresentation;
- Violations of other applicable law, including privacy law.

### Section 3 — Expansion of High-Risk AI Interaction Categories

SB 226 expands the categories of AI interactions classified as "high-risk" under the Utah AI Policy Act, for which disclosure requirements are most stringent. The expanded definition includes interactions involving:

1. Medical diagnosis, treatment recommendations, or clinical decision support communicated directly to a patient or consumer;
2. Legal advice or document preparation in matters affecting a person's legal rights, obligations, or status;
3. Financial advice involving investment decisions, retirement planning, or the assessment of insurance needs;
4. Mental or behavioral health support or counseling;
5. **New under SB 226:** Employment decisions, including hiring recommendations, performance evaluation, or termination;
6. **New under SB 226:** Educational assessment, grading, or academic progress determinations affecting students;
7. **New under SB 226:** Housing recommendations or credit/lending decisions affecting where or how a person may live.

Regulated persons in high-risk categories must, in addition to clear and conspicuous disclosure:
- Inform the consumer of their right to request a human review of any AI-assisted decision;
- Retain records of AI-assisted interactions for 24 months;
- Designate a responsible human professional who can be contacted with questions or concerns about AI use.

### Section 4 — Regulated Occupation Clarifications

SB 226 clarifies which licensed professions are subject to the AI disclosure requirements under Utah Code § 13-2-8:

**Expressly covered professions include:**
- Licensed physicians, nurses, and other healthcare providers licensed under Utah Code Title 58;
- Licensed attorneys and other legal service providers;
- Licensed financial advisers, insurance agents, and mortgage loan originators;
- Licensed real estate agents and brokers;
- Licensed mental health professionals (counselors, social workers, marriage and family therapists);
- Licensed educators in regulated educational settings.

**The following are clarified as not subject to the Act:**
- Internal business use of AI tools not involving direct consumer interaction;
- AI used solely for administrative or clerical functions;
- AI tools provided by a licensed professional to another licensed professional, where both parties understand the AI context.

### Section 5 — Consumer Inquiry Right

SB 226 codifies the right of any consumer, in any interaction with a regulated person, to ask:
- Whether AI is being used in the interaction;
- What the AI's role is;
- Whether a human professional reviewed AI-generated outputs before they were communicated to the consumer.

A regulated person must respond truthfully and promptly to such an inquiry. Failure to respond or a false response is a violation of the Act.

## Enforcement

### Utah Division of Consumer Protection

The Utah Division of Consumer Protection (DCP) retains primary enforcement authority under SB 226. The DCP may:
- Investigate complaints from consumers regarding AI disclosure violations;
- Issue civil investigative demands;
- Assess civil penalties of up to **$5,000 per violation** for violations of the clear and conspicuous disclosure standard;
- Refer matters involving licensed professionals to the applicable licensing board for concurrent disciplinary proceedings.

### Professional Licensing Boards

Each professional licensing board may enforce compliance with SB 226 within its licensed profession through:
- License suspension or revocation;
- Probation with mandatory compliance training;
- Civil fines within the board's statutory authority.

### No Private Right of Action

SB 226 does not create a private right of action. Enforcement is exclusively through the DCP and applicable licensing boards. However, consumers may file complaints with the DCP, which the DCP is required to investigate within 90 days of receipt.

## Relationship to the 2024 AI Policy Act (SB 149)

SB 226 amends but does not repeal SB 149. The two laws should be read together as a unified framework. Key differences:

| Feature | SB 149 (2024) | SB 226 (2025) |
|---|---|---|
| Disclosure standard | General "disclosure" | "Clear and conspicuous" |
| Safe harbor | Not explicit | Codified |
| High-risk categories | 4 categories | 7 categories (3 added) |
| Consumer inquiry right | Not explicit | Codified |
| Record retention | Not specified | 24 months for high-risk |

## Significance

Utah SB 226 is significant as a model of iterative AI governance: a legislature returning to an enacted AI law within one year to refine its standards based on implementation experience. The codified "clear and conspicuous" safe harbor provides regulated professionals with a clear compliance path, while the expansion of high-risk categories reflects evolving understanding of where AI causes the greatest consumer harm risk. Utah continues to lead among US states in building a practical, enforceable AI consumer protection framework.
