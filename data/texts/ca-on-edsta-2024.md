# Strengthening Cyber Security and Building Trust in the Public Sector Act, 2024

## Ontario Bill 194 — Comprehensive Overview

**Full Title:** Strengthening Cyber Security and Building Trust in the Public Sector Act, 2024
**Bill Number:** Bill 194 (43rd Parliament, 1st Session)
**Royal Assent:** 25 November 2024
**Chapter:** Chapter 24, Statutes of Ontario 2024
**Responsible Ministry:** Ministry of Public and Business Service Delivery and Procurement
**Official Citation:** S.O. 2024, c. 24

**Note on Constituent Acts:** Bill 194 enacts two new statutes and amends several existing ones:
1. **Schedule 1 — Enhancing Digital Security and Trust Act ("EDSTA"):** New statute addressing AI systems and cybersecurity in the public sector.
2. **Schedule 2 — Freedom of Information and Protection of Privacy Act ("FIPPA") amendments:** Strengthening personal information protection for public sector institutions.

---

## Overview and Policy Context

Ontario's Bill 194 represents Canada's first AI-specific legislative framework for public sector institutions, establishing mandatory requirements for how provincial and municipal government entities, school boards, children's aid societies, and other public bodies must approach the use of artificial intelligence systems. It addresses two interconnected areas of digital risk:

1. **Cybersecurity:** Requiring public sector entities to have cybersecurity programs, report cyber incidents, and manage cyber risks in a systematic manner.

2. **Artificial Intelligence Governance:** Requiring public sector entities that use (or intend to use) AI systems to be transparent about that use, implement accountability frameworks, manage associated risks, and ensure meaningful human oversight.

Bill 194 was introduced in May 2024, passed Third Reading and received Royal Assent on November 25, 2024, making Ontario a pioneer in public sector AI regulation in Canada. The legislation responds to rapid AI adoption in Ontario's public sector, concerns about accountability in automated decision-making affecting residents' access to public services, and rising cybersecurity threats targeting government infrastructure.

The Act does not impose immediate detailed compliance obligations — rather, it establishes the legislative framework and grants authority for regulations that will specify the precise requirements. The provincial government has signaled a phased regulatory rollout.

## Schedule 1: Enhancing Digital Security and Trust Act (EDSTA)

### Part I — Interpretation and Application

**Definition of Artificial Intelligence System (Section 1)**

The EDSTA adopts the OECD definition of AI systems, consistent with the EU AI Act and proposed amendments to Canada's federal Artificial Intelligence and Data Act (AIDA). An "artificial intelligence system" is defined as:

> "a machine-based system that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers from the input it receives in order to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments, and such other systems as may be prescribed."

This definition is intentionally broad and technology-neutral, designed to remain relevant as AI technology evolves. It covers a wide range of systems including:
- Machine learning models (supervised, unsupervised, reinforcement learning);
- Large language models and generative AI systems;
- Computer vision and image recognition systems;
- Predictive analytics and risk scoring tools;
- Natural language processing and chatbots;
- Optimization and planning algorithms.

The definition can be expanded by regulation ("such other systems as may be prescribed"), allowing the government to keep the definition current.

**Definition of Cybersecurity Incident (Section 1)**

A "cybersecurity incident" is defined as a real or suspected act or omission that compromises, or may compromise, the security, confidentiality, integrity or availability of an electronic information system or the information that the system stores, processes or communicates.

**Entities Subject to the Act (Sections 2–3)**

The EDSTA applies to:

1. **Institutions within the meaning of FIPPA:** Government ministries, agencies, and other provincial public sector bodies subject to the Freedom of Information and Protection of Privacy Act.

2. **Institutions within the meaning of MFIPPA:** Municipal institutions, including municipalities, local boards, and commissions.

3. **Children's Aid Societies:** Specified under the Child, Youth and Family Services Act.

4. **School Boards:** Boards of education within the meaning of the Education Act.

**AI provisions have a narrower scope:** The AI-specific requirements in sections 11–15 apply only to entities that **use or intend to use AI systems in prescribed circumstances**. The prescribed circumstances — to be determined by regulation — are expected to focus on significant decision-making, direct service delivery, and other high-impact uses of AI.

### Part II — Cybersecurity Requirements (Sections 4–10)

**Cybersecurity Programs (Section 4)**

Regulations may require institutions to establish, implement, maintain, and document cybersecurity programs. A cybersecurity program must address:

- Governance and accountability for cybersecurity within the institution;
- Risk assessment and risk management for cybersecurity threats;
- Technical and organizational controls for preventing, detecting, and responding to cyber incidents;
- Business continuity and recovery planning;
- Employee training and awareness;
- Third-party and vendor risk management;
- Compliance monitoring and testing.

**Cybersecurity Incident Reporting (Sections 5–7)**

The Act authorizes regulations requiring institutions to report cybersecurity incidents to the provincial government (through a designated reporting mechanism). Reporting obligations may include:

- Initial notification within a specified time of detecting a significant cyber incident;
- Follow-up reports with technical details, impact assessment, and remediation steps;
- Post-incident reviews and lessons-learned documentation.

The reporting framework is intended to enable the provincial government to monitor the threat landscape, provide assistance to affected institutions, and share anonymized threat intelligence across the public sector.

**Cybersecurity Assessments (Sections 8–9)**

The Act authorizes regulations requiring institutions to conduct periodic cybersecurity assessments, which may include:

- Risk assessments identifying cyber threats, vulnerabilities, and potential impacts;
- Maturity assessments against recognized cybersecurity frameworks (such as the NIST Cybersecurity Framework or CIS Controls);
- Third-party penetration testing and vulnerability assessments.

**Designation of Chief Information Security Officer (Section 10)**

Large institutions may be required by regulation to designate a Chief Information Security Officer (CISO) or equivalent senior official with responsibility for the institution's cybersecurity program and reporting directly to senior management.

### Part III — Artificial Intelligence Requirements (Sections 11–17)

**Core Framework — Transparency, Accountability, and Risk Management**

Section 11 establishes the three-pillar framework for public sector AI governance:

1. **Transparency:** Institutions using AI systems in prescribed circumstances must disclose their use of those systems to the public. Disclosure must be made in a clear, accessible manner.

2. **Accountability:** Institutions must develop and implement an accountability framework for their use of AI systems. The accountability framework must address governance, oversight, and responsibility for AI-related decisions and outcomes.

3. **Risk Management:** Institutions must take steps to manage risks associated with their use of AI systems, including technical, ethical, legal, and operational risks.

**Mandatory AI Oversight Officer (Section 12)**

Institutions that use or intend to use AI systems in prescribed circumstances must designate an individual within the institution as responsible for oversight of AI systems. This person (who may hold various titles, such as Chief AI Officer, AI Accountability Officer, or similar) is responsible for:

- Overseeing the institution's compliance with AI requirements under the Act and regulations;
- Ensuring that accountability frameworks are developed, implemented, and reviewed;
- Serving as the point of contact for the public and for regulators on AI-related matters;
- Reporting to senior management on AI risks, incidents, and compliance status.

The designation of an individual for AI oversight is mandatory — it is not conditional on regulations specifying prescribed circumstances. This creates an immediate structural governance obligation for all institutions that use AI systems.

**Public Disclosure Obligations (Section 13)**

Institutions must provide information to the public about their use of AI systems in prescribed circumstances. The specific content and format of required disclosures will be determined by regulation, but may include:

- A description of the AI systems being used;
- The purposes for which the AI systems are used;
- The types of decisions or recommendations the AI systems generate;
- The categories of individuals whose information is used or affected;
- The safeguards and human oversight measures in place;
- How individuals can obtain more information or request human review of AI-influenced decisions affecting them.

This disclosure requirement reflects the principle that individuals who interact with or are affected by AI-assisted government decision-making are entitled to know about that use.

**Accountability Frameworks (Section 14)**

Each institution must develop and implement an accountability framework for its AI use. The framework must be tailored to the institution's specific AI applications and risks. While regulatory specifics are pending, accountability frameworks for public sector AI are expected to address:

- **Governance structure:** Who is responsible for AI decisions, at what level of authority, and how responsibility is allocated;
- **Procurement standards:** How the institution evaluates, selects, and contracts for AI systems, including vendor due diligence;
- **Risk assessment processes:** How the institution identifies, assesses, and mitigates risks from AI use;
- **Human oversight mechanisms:** How human review and decision-making authority is maintained over AI-generated recommendations and outputs;
- **Fairness and equity audits:** How the institution ensures AI systems do not produce discriminatory or inequitable outcomes;
- **Monitoring and evaluation:** How the institution monitors AI system performance and identifies problems;
- **Incident response:** How the institution responds when an AI system produces incorrect, unfair, or harmful outputs;
- **Review and update cycles:** How frequently the framework and AI systems are reviewed and updated.

**Risk Management (Section 15)**

Beyond the accountability framework, institutions must take active steps to manage specific risks associated with their AI systems. Risk management obligations may include:

- **Bias and fairness testing:** Testing AI systems for discriminatory bias, particularly with respect to protected characteristics under the Ontario Human Rights Code (race, Indigenous identity, disability, age, sex, gender identity, sexual orientation, etc.);
- **Data quality:** Ensuring that data used to train and operate AI systems is accurate, representative, and fit for purpose;
- **Security:** Ensuring AI systems are protected against adversarial attacks, data poisoning, and unauthorized access;
- **Reliability and accuracy:** Testing AI systems for accuracy and reliability before deployment and monitoring during use;
- **Over-reliance prevention:** Ensuring that human decision-makers do not inappropriately defer to AI recommendations without adequate scrutiny.

**AI in Decision-Making Affecting Individuals (Section 15.1)**

Where AI systems are used in processes that result in decisions affecting individual Ontarians — such as eligibility for benefits, access to services, licensing, or enforcement actions — additional safeguards apply:

- Individuals must be informed when their matter has been handled with AI assistance;
- Individuals must have a right to human review of AI-influenced decisions affecting them;
- Decision-makers must retain meaningful authority to deviate from AI recommendations;
- Records must be maintained of AI system outputs and the human decisions made in relation to them.

### Part IV — Regulations (Sections 18–22)

The EDSTA grants the Lieutenant Governor in Council broad regulation-making authority, including authority to:

- Prescribe circumstances in which the AI and cybersecurity requirements apply;
- Prescribe the content and format of public disclosures about AI use;
- Prescribe the content of accountability frameworks;
- Prescribe risk management requirements;
- Designate categories of institution subject to particular requirements;
- Set timelines for compliance;
- Provide exemptions from requirements for specified entities or AI uses;
- Establish requirements for reporting to the Minister on AI and cybersecurity matters;
- Define terms and expand definitions (including the definition of AI systems).

The regulation-making power is central to the Act's implementation — it creates a flexible framework that can be specified and updated as the regulatory landscape evolves.

## Schedule 2: FIPPA Amendments

Bill 194 also amends the Freedom of Information and Protection of Privacy Act (FIPPA) to strengthen privacy protections for personal information held by public sector institutions. Key amendments include:

**Enhanced Privacy Impact Assessments:**
Institutions must conduct Privacy Impact Assessments (PIAs) for programs and activities that involve the collection, use, or disclosure of personal information, with particular emphasis on programs that use new technologies, including AI. PIAs must be documented and, in significant cases, provided to the Information and Privacy Commissioner for review.

**Mandatory Breach Notification:**
The amendments strengthen requirements for institutions to report privacy breaches — unauthorized access to, collection of, use, disclosure, copying, modification, retention, or disposal of personal information — to the Information and Privacy Commissioner and, where appropriate, to affected individuals.

**Data Minimisation Obligations:**
Institutions must not collect personal information beyond what is necessary for the purpose for which it is collected, and must delete personal information when it is no longer needed.

**Third-Party Accountability:**
Where institutions share personal information with service providers, contractors, or other third parties, they must ensure that appropriate data protection agreements are in place and that personal information is protected to the same standard as if it were held directly by the institution.

**Enhanced Powers of the Information and Privacy Commissioner:**
The IPC receives enhanced authority to audit institutions' compliance with FIPPA, to issue binding orders, and to impose administrative penalties for serious or repeated violations.

## Relationship to Other Canadian AI Laws

**Federal AIDA (proposed):** The federal Artificial Intelligence and Data Act, introduced as part of Bill C-27 in June 2022, proposes a federal AI regulatory framework for high-impact AI systems. If enacted, AIDA would apply to AI systems in areas of federal jurisdiction (banking, telecommunications, aviation, interprovincial trade). Ontario's EDSTA applies to provincial and municipal public sector entities, creating a complementary (rather than competing) framework.

**Quebec Law 25:** Quebec enacted comprehensive personal data protection legislation (Law 25, amending the Act Respecting the Protection of Personal Information in the Private Sector) in stages from 2022 to 2023. Like FIPPA, it addresses personal data protection but does not contain AI-specific governance requirements for the public sector comparable to EDSTA.

**Ontario MFIPPA:** The Municipal Freedom of Information and Protection of Privacy Act (MFIPPA) applies to municipal institutions in Ontario and is subject to the same amendments as FIPPA under Schedule 2 of Bill 194.

## Implementation Status and Next Steps

As of the date of this entry, the EDSTA and FIPPA amendments have received Royal Assent (November 25, 2024) but the specific provisions requiring regulations to prescribe circumstances, content, and timelines are awaiting those regulations. The provincial government has indicated a phased implementation approach:

**Immediate obligations (in force upon Royal Assent):**
- The obligation to designate an individual responsible for AI oversight (Section 12) applies to all institutions that use AI systems, without waiting for prescribed circumstances to be defined.

**Phased obligations (subject to regulations):**
- Public disclosure requirements;
- Accountability framework content requirements;
- Risk management specific requirements;
- Cybersecurity program and reporting requirements;
- Prescribed circumstances triggering the full AI requirements.

The Information and Privacy Commissioner of Ontario has published guidance encouraging institutions to begin preparing for compliance while regulations are developed, and has noted its intention to engage with institutions on AI governance best practices.

## Significance

Ontario's Strengthening Cyber Security and Building Trust in the Public Sector Act, 2024 is significant for several reasons:

1. **First in Canada:** EDSTA establishes Canada's first AI-specific regulatory requirements for public sector institutions at the provincial level, ahead of any comparable federal legislation taking effect.

2. **OECD AI Definition:** Ontario's adoption of the OECD definition of AI systems positions it for future harmonization with federal law and international frameworks.

3. **Immediate governance requirement:** The mandatory designation of an AI oversight official takes effect without waiting for regulations, creating an immediate institutional accountability structure.

4. **Flexibility for evolution:** The regulation-driven implementation model allows requirements to be specified and updated as best practices develop and AI technology evolves.

5. **Model for other provinces:** Ontario's framework may serve as a template for similar legislation in other Canadian provinces, particularly as AI adoption in the public sector accelerates.
