# UN General Assembly Resolution A/RES/78/265

## Seizing the Opportunities of Safe, Secure and Trustworthy Artificial Intelligence Systems for Sustainable Development

**Adopted:** 21 March 2024 (78th Session, 61st Plenary Meeting)
**Introduced by:** United States of America, co-sponsored by over 120 Member States
**Adopted by:** Consensus (without a vote)
**Document Symbol:** A/RES/78/265

---

## Background and Significance

Resolution A/RES/78/265 is the first standalone resolution on artificial intelligence adopted by the United Nations General Assembly. It marks a watershed moment in global AI governance, establishing that the international community — including states with divergent approaches to technology regulation such as China and Russia — can reach consensus on foundational principles for the responsible development and deployment of AI systems.

The resolution was introduced as document A/78/L.49 and adopted at the 61st plenary meeting of the 78th session of the General Assembly on 21 March 2024. More than 120 Member States co-sponsored the resolution prior to its adoption, reflecting the broad political support for international cooperation on AI governance. Notably, the resolution was adopted without objection from China or Russia, underscoring its carefully calibrated balance between innovation-enabling principles and human rights protections.

The resolution emerged in the context of rapid advancements in generative AI systems and growing international debate about AI's potential to both accelerate and impede progress toward the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development. It builds on prior work by UNESCO (the Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence, 2021), the OECD AI Principles (2019), and the G20's AI Principles, while extending those frameworks to the UN's universal membership.

---

## Preambular Provisions

The resolution's preamble establishes the normative framework and factual basis for its operative provisions. Key preambular elements include:

**Reaffirming** existing UN commitments including the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, the Sustainable Development Goals, and the commitment that no one and no country should be left behind in global development.

**Recognizing** that artificial intelligence technologies have the potential to accelerate the achievement of the Sustainable Development Goals while also presenting risks that need to be carefully managed, including risks to human rights, safety, and security.

**Acknowledging** that the development and deployment of AI systems could exacerbate existing inequalities between developed and developing countries, between urban and rural populations, and between different demographic groups within countries, if not governed inclusively.

**Recalling** the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and international human rights law, and affirming that the same rights that people have offline must also be protected online, including in the context of AI systems.

**Noting with concern** the digital divide that exists between and within countries, including disparities in access to computing infrastructure, data, technical expertise, and financial resources needed to develop and benefit from AI systems.

**Emphasizing** that data is fundamental to the development and operation of artificial intelligence systems, and that fair, inclusive, responsible and effective data governance — including improving data generation, accessibility and infrastructure, and the use of digital public goods — is essential to harnessing the potential of safe, secure and trustworthy AI for sustainable development.

**Taking note** of efforts by UNESCO, the OECD, the G20, the G7, the Council of Europe, the European Union, the African Union, and regional and national bodies to develop standards, guidelines, and regulatory frameworks for AI.

**Emphasizing** that AI systems should be designed, developed, deployed and used in ways that respect, protect and promote human rights and fundamental freedoms, including by being transparent, explainable, fair, non-discriminatory, privacy-preserving, secure, and accountable.

## Operative Provisions

### Section I: Core Principles for Safe, Secure and Trustworthy AI

The resolution **calls upon** all Member States, international organizations, the private sector, civil society, and other relevant stakeholders to work cooperatively to promote the design, development, deployment and use of safe, secure and trustworthy AI systems. The resolution articulates the following foundational principles:

1. **Human-centeredness:** AI systems should be designed and deployed in ways that place human wellbeing, dignity, and rights at the center, treating humans as ends rather than means.

2. **Safety and security:** AI systems should function as intended without causing unintended harm, and should include safeguards against misuse, adversarial attacks, and unintended consequences.

3. **Trustworthiness:** AI systems should be reliable, transparent, explainable, and subject to human oversight and accountability mechanisms.

4. **Fairness and non-discrimination:** AI systems should not perpetuate or amplify discrimination based on race, ethnicity, gender, disability, national origin, religion, or other protected characteristics.

5. **Privacy and data protection:** The development and use of AI systems must respect individuals' right to privacy and comply with applicable data protection laws and principles.

6. **Accountability:** Those who develop, deploy, and use AI systems should be accountable for their outcomes, with clear responsibility chains and effective remediation mechanisms.

7. **Transparency and explainability:** Decisions made or influenced by AI systems should be explicable in ways that affected individuals and oversight bodies can understand.

8. **Inclusiveness:** The benefits of AI should be accessible to all people, with particular attention to historically marginalized and underserved communities.

### Section II: Sustainable Development and the Digital Divide

The resolution **urges** Member States and relevant stakeholders to cooperate to ensure that developing countries are able to harness the benefits of AI for sustainable development. Specific commitments include:

- **Capacity building:** Developed countries and international organizations should provide technical assistance, training, and institutional capacity building to developing countries to enable them to develop their own AI strategies and regulatory capabilities.

- **Technology transfer:** On mutually agreed terms, encouraging technology transfer and knowledge sharing to help developing countries participate in the global AI ecosystem, not merely as consumers but as developers and innovators.

- **Infrastructure investment:** Recognizing that meaningful AI participation requires foundational digital infrastructure, including reliable internet connectivity, data centers, and computational resources, the resolution calls for increased investment in such infrastructure in developing countries.

- **Digital public goods:** Encouraging the development and sharing of open-source AI models, datasets, and tools as digital public goods that can be freely used and adapted by developing countries.

- **Multilingualism:** AI systems and datasets should reflect the world's linguistic diversity, not privilege a small number of dominant languages, to ensure equitable access across cultures.

- **Financial mechanisms:** Exploring innovative financing, including through multilateral development banks and the UN system, to support AI-related development in lower-income countries.

The resolution specifically **notes** the risk that concentration of AI development in a small number of countries and companies could entrench existing global inequalities and calls for measures to prevent such concentration from foreclosing opportunities for developing nations.

### Section III: Governance and International Cooperation

The resolution **encourages** the development of international frameworks for AI governance that are:

- **Inclusive and multilateral:** Governance discussions should take place in forums where all countries have meaningful voice, not just a small set of technologically advanced nations.

- **Multi-stakeholder:** Effective AI governance requires participation from governments, the private sector, civil society, academia, and technical communities.

- **Interoperable:** National and regional AI regulations should be designed with interoperability in mind, to avoid fragmentation that could impede beneficial cross-border uses of AI.

- **Adaptive:** Given the rapid pace of AI development, governance frameworks should be regularly reviewed and updated to remain current.

The resolution **requests** the Secretary-General to convene multi-stakeholder consultations and to report to the General Assembly on the opportunities and challenges posed by AI systems for sustainable development.

The resolution **decides** to continue consideration of issues related to AI systems for sustainable development at future sessions of the General Assembly.

### Section IV: Human Rights and Rule of Law

The resolution **reaffirms** that AI systems must operate within the framework of international human rights law and the rule of law. It specifically calls for:

- **Human rights impact assessments:** Encouraging the use of human rights impact assessments before deploying AI systems in sensitive contexts, particularly those that could affect civil liberties, due process, or equal treatment under law.

- **Prohibition on harmful applications:** Calling on states and non-state actors to refrain from developing or deploying AI systems that would violate international human rights law or international humanitarian law.

- **Effective remedies:** Ensuring that individuals harmed by AI systems have access to effective legal remedies and redress mechanisms.

- **Non-surveillance state uses:** While not prohibiting AI-assisted surveillance, the resolution emphasizes that mass surveillance applications must comply with international human rights standards and the principles of necessity, proportionality, and legality.

- **Autonomous weapons:** While not resolving the contested question of lethal autonomous weapons systems, noting ongoing discussions in relevant forums and the importance of ensuring meaningful human control over decisions to use force.

### Section V: AI Safety Research

The resolution **encourages** investment in AI safety research, specifically including:

- Research into technical methods for making AI systems more interpretable, robust, and aligned with human values.
- Development of evaluation frameworks and benchmarks for assessing AI safety.
- International cooperation on AI safety research, including information sharing about risks and incidents.
- Investment in frontier AI safety by leading AI developers, including measures to identify and mitigate catastrophic or irreversible risks from advanced AI systems.

### Section VI: Role of the UN System

The resolution **invites** UN agencies, funds, and programs to integrate AI governance considerations into their mandates, and to support Member States in developing national AI strategies and regulatory frameworks. Specific roles identified include:

- **UNESCO:** Leading on AI ethics, education, and cultural diversity aspects of AI governance.
- **ITU:** Leading on technical standards, connectivity, and digital infrastructure.
- **UNCTAD:** Addressing AI's implications for trade, development, and economic inequality.
- **OHCHR:** Monitoring the human rights implications of AI systems.
- **UNDP:** Supporting national AI strategies and capacity building in developing countries.

## Relationship to Subsequent UN AI Governance Actions

Resolution A/RES/78/265 was followed by several related developments at the UN:

- **Resolution A/RES/78/311** (July 2024): A second General Assembly resolution on AI governance, focusing on international cooperation on AI capacity building, adopted during the 78th session.

- **Summit of the Future (September 2024):** The "Pact for the Future," adopted at the Summit of the Future, included commitments on AI governance building on the March resolution.

- **High-Level Advisory Body on AI:** The Secretary-General's High-Level Advisory Body on AI, established prior to the resolution, continued its work and published its final report "Governing AI for Humanity" in September 2024, recommending the creation of an International Panel on AI Safety and other multilateral governance mechanisms.

- **Global Digital Compact:** Negotiations on the Global Digital Compact, concluded at the Summit of the Future, incorporated AI governance principles consistent with Resolution A/RES/78/265.

## Significance and Analysis

Resolution A/RES/78/265 is significant for several reasons:

1. **Universality:** It establishes AI governance as a matter of concern for all UN Member States, not just those with advanced AI industries, and affirms that all countries should have a voice in shaping global norms.

2. **Consensus:** The unanimous adoption signals that even geopolitical rivals can agree on foundational AI principles, creating a potential basis for more specific international agreements.

3. **Development focus:** The resolution's strong emphasis on the digital divide and the needs of developing countries distinguishes it from some other AI governance frameworks that focus primarily on risks in advanced economies.

4. **Non-binding but normatively significant:** While UN General Assembly resolutions are not legally binding, they reflect the consensus views of the international community and carry significant normative weight in shaping national policies and corporate behavior.

5. **Platform for future action:** The resolution creates a framework for ongoing UN engagement with AI governance and establishes expectations that the Secretary-General and UN system will continue to engage with these issues.

## Key Definitions and Concepts

The resolution uses the term "artificial intelligence systems" broadly, consistent with emerging international consensus (including the OECD definition) to mean machine-based systems that, for given objectives, can generate outputs such as predictions, recommendations, decisions, or content that influence real or virtual environments.

The phrase "safe, secure and trustworthy" AI — central to the resolution's title — reflects the framework promoted by the United States and other leading AI nations, and is echoed in the G7 Hiroshima AI Process outcomes and the EU AI Act's foundational principles.

"Sustainable development" in the resolution's context refers specifically to the 2030 Agenda and the 17 Sustainable Development Goals, framing AI governance not merely as a technology policy question but as a development policy imperative.
