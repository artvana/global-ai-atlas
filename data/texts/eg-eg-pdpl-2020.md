## Summary and Analysis

### Purpose and Scope

Egypt's Law No. 151 of 2020 on Personal Data Protection, promulgated on 15 July 2020 and accompanied by Executive Regulations issued in 2021, represents Egypt's first comprehensive legislative framework governing the collection, processing, storage, transfer, and use of personal data. Prior to Law 151/2020, data protection in Egypt was addressed only through scattered sector-specific provisions in laws governing banking, telecommunications, and electronic transactions, and through general constitutional provisions recognizing privacy as a fundamental right. Law 151/2020 fills that gap with a unified, modern framework influenced by international standards including the GDPR, though adapted to Egypt's legal traditions and administrative context.

The Law applies to all processing of personal data carried out wholly or partly within Egypt, to any controller established in Egypt regardless of where the processing occurs, and to any controller established outside Egypt that processes personal data of persons residing in Egypt in connection with offering goods or services to those persons or monitoring their behavior within Egypt. The Law covers both automated and non-automated processing of personal data contained in structured filing systems.

The Law applies to private sector entities and, to a qualified extent, to public sector bodies. Processing by government agencies for national security, public order, and law enforcement purposes is subject to reduced disclosure obligations and certain exemptions, consistent with Egypt's constitutional framework and the sensitive nature of such processing. Processing for journalistic, academic, artistic, and literary purposes is also subject to modified requirements provided such processing is conducted in a manner consistent with applicable professional and ethical standards.

The Law establishes the **Personal Data Protection Authority (PDPLA)** as the regulatory body responsible for oversight, enforcement, and international cooperation. The PDPLA operates under the Ministry of Communications and Information Technology and is administered by a board of directors with representatives from relevant ministries and independent experts.

### Key Definitions

**Personal data** means any data relating to an identified or identifiable natural person, including name, voice, picture, identification number, address, or electronic identifier, or any factor specific to the physical, psychological, economic, cultural, social, or political identity of that person.

**Sensitive personal data** (al-bayanat al-shakhsiyya al-hassassa) includes: data relating to racial or ethnic origin; religious beliefs; political opinions; criminal records; health data; biometric data; genetic data; data about financial transactions; and any other category of data that the PDPLA designates as sensitive by regulation.

**Data controller** (al-mutahhakkim fi al-bayanat) means the natural or legal person, public authority, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of processing personal data.

**Data processor** (al-mu'alij) means the natural or legal person, public authority, or body that processes personal data on behalf of and under the instructions of the controller.

**Data subject** (sahib al-bayanat) means the natural person to whom the personal data relates.

**Consent** must be given freely, specifically, with full awareness, and unambiguously. Implicit or inferred consent does not satisfy the Law's requirements. Consent obtained under duress, deception, or excessive incentivization is void. Where consent is required, the controller must retain evidence that valid consent was obtained.

### Core Obligations for Data Controllers and Processors

**Lawful Processing Grounds.** The Law requires processing to be grounded in one of the following bases: explicit consent of the data subject; performance of a contract to which the data subject is a party; compliance with a legal obligation; protection of vital interests; exercise of official authority or performance of a public interest task; or the legitimate interest of the controller or third parties, subject to non-override of the data subject's fundamental rights.

**Transparency — Privacy Notice Obligations.** Controllers must provide data subjects with comprehensive privacy notices at the time of data collection or prior to first use of previously collected data. The notice must disclose: the controller's identity and contact details; the purposes and legal bases for processing; the categories of data collected and processed; recipients or categories of recipients; international transfers and safeguards; retention periods; the data subject's rights and how to exercise them; the existence of any automated decision-making; and the right to withdraw consent.

**Purpose Limitation and Data Minimization.** Processing must be confined to the specified purposes. Data collected must be limited to what is necessary for those purposes, must be accurate and updated, and must not be retained beyond the period necessary.

**Security Measures.** Controllers must implement appropriate technical and organizational security measures to protect personal data against unauthorized access, disclosure, modification, or destruction. The level of security must be proportionate to the nature of the data, the scale of processing, and the likely severity of harm from a breach. The PDPLA may issue binding technical security standards.

**Data Breach Notification.** Controllers are required to notify the PDPLA of personal data breaches that may pose a risk to data subjects' rights and freedoms. Notification must include a description of the breach, the categories and approximate number of data subjects affected, the categories and approximate number of records concerned, the contact details of the data protection officer or other contact point, the likely consequences of the breach, and the measures taken or proposed to address it. High-risk breaches must also be communicated to affected data subjects.

**Data Protection Officer.** Controllers engaged in large-scale processing of sensitive personal data, or whose core activities involve regular and systematic monitoring of data subjects at large scale, are required to appoint a Data Protection Officer. The DPO must be registered with the PDPLA.

**Processor Agreements.** Processing by a processor must be governed by a binding written agreement imposing obligations on the processor consistent with the Law, including restrictions on sub-processing and obligations to notify the controller of breaches.

**Record-Keeping.** Controllers must maintain records of processing activities sufficient to demonstrate compliance with the Law. These records must be made available to the PDPLA upon request.

### Data Residency Requirements

A distinctive and significant feature of Egypt's Law 151/2020 is its data residency requirement. The Law requires that personal data of Egyptian citizens and residents be **stored on servers physically located within Egypt**. This localization requirement applies as a default for all personal data of Egyptian data subjects, with exceptions available only through the cross-border transfer framework described below. The data residency requirement reflects the Egyptian government's concerns about national sovereignty over data, access by foreign intelligence agencies, and the country's strategic interest in building a domestic data center industry.

Controllers must ensure that their primary data storage infrastructure is located within Egypt, or must obtain PDPLA authorization for storage abroad. Cloud computing arrangements must either route data through Egyptian-domiciled infrastructure or comply with the cross-border transfer authorization requirements.

### Cross-Border Transfer Rules

International transfers of personal data from Egypt are subject to a comprehensive authorization regime. Personal data may be transferred to a third country or international organization only where:

- The PDPLA has determined that the recipient country provides an adequate level of protection;
- The controller has provided appropriate safeguards such as standard contractual clauses approved by the PDPLA, binding corporate rules, or adherence to an approved certification scheme;
- A specific derogation applies: the data subject has given explicit informed consent; the transfer is necessary for contract performance; the transfer is necessary for legal claims; the transfer is necessary to protect vital interests; or the transfer is for recognized international humanitarian purposes.

Notably, certain categories of data designated as "critical" by the PDPLA may be subject to absolute localization requirements, meaning they may not be transferred abroad under any circumstances. The PDPLA has indicated that categories such as national health records, civil registry data, and electoral data fall within this category.

Controllers seeking to transfer data that does not fall within an exemption must apply to the PDPLA for a transfer permit. The PDPLA reviews such applications against criteria including the purpose of the transfer, the adequacy of protection in the recipient country, and the contractual safeguards in place.

### Automated Decision-Making Provisions

Law 151/2020 includes provisions on automated decision-making that, while less detailed than the GDPR Article 22 framework, establish meaningful protections for data subjects. Data subjects have the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing — including profiling — that produces legal effects or similarly significantly affects them.

This right applies particularly in the context of decisions about financial services (including credit assessment and insurance pricing), employment, health care, and access to public services. Exceptions apply where: the decision is necessary for performance of a contract; the decision is authorized by law; or the data subject has explicitly consented. Where automated decisions are permitted, the controller must: inform the data subject that the decision was made by automated means; provide meaningful information about the logic involved; allow the data subject to request human review; provide the data subject with the opportunity to express their view; and allow the data subject to contest the decision.

The Executive Regulations issued by the PDPLA elaborate on the obligations for controllers using automated systems, including requirements for documentation of the decision logic, periodic review of automated systems for accuracy and fairness, and the maintenance of human-in-the-loop mechanisms for high-impact automated decisions.

### Rights of Data Subjects

The Law grants data subjects the following rights:

**Right of Access.** The right to obtain from the controller confirmation of whether personal data is being processed, and if so, access to that data and related information.

**Right to Correction.** The right to have inaccurate or incomplete personal data corrected or completed without undue delay.

**Right to Erasure.** The right to have personal data deleted where it is no longer necessary, where consent has been withdrawn and no other basis applies, where processing was unlawful, or where deletion is required by law.

**Right to Restriction.** The right to restrict processing in specified circumstances, including while accuracy is contested or an objection is pending.

**Right to Portability.** The right to receive personal data in a structured, commonly used, machine-readable format and to transmit it to another controller, where technically feasible.

**Right to Object.** The right to object to processing based on legitimate interests or for direct marketing purposes.

**Right to Human Review.** The right to request that automated decisions be reviewed by a human being.

**Right to Withdraw Consent.** The right to withdraw consent at any time without prejudice to the lawfulness of prior processing.

Controllers must respond to data subject requests within 30 days.

### Enforcement and Penalties

The PDPLA has broad enforcement powers including the ability to conduct inspections, require production of documents and information, issue compliance orders, and impose administrative fines. The Law also provides for criminal penalties for serious violations.

**Administrative Penalties.** The PDPLA may impose fines for violations of the Law's requirements. Maximum administrative fines are:

- For processing personal data without legal basis, failing to obtain consent, or violating data subject rights: up to EGP 1,000,000 (approximately USD 20,000).
- For violations involving sensitive personal data, failing to implement security measures, or transferring data internationally without authorization: up to EGP 5,000,000 (approximately USD 100,000).
- For repeat violations or violations involving the data of a large number of individuals: up to EGP 10,000,000 (approximately USD 200,000).

**Criminal Penalties.** The Law establishes criminal liability for the most serious violations. Controllers or individuals that process personal data without authorization, disclose sensitive data without consent, or use personal data for purposes of blackmail or causing harm face imprisonment of up to one year and/or fines. Where the violation involves financial gain or organized criminal activity, penalties may be more severe. Legal persons may also be subject to criminal liability through their officers.

The Law establishes a graduated approach to enforcement: the PDPLA is required to first issue a warning and allow the controller a period to remedy the violation before imposing fines, except in cases of serious or repeated violations or where immediate action is necessary to protect data subjects.

Egypt's PDPLA is in the process of building institutional capacity and issuing implementing regulations across various areas of the Law. Enforcement activity has focused initially on major private sector entities in telecommunications, banking, and e-commerce, with the PDPLA publicizing enforcement actions to raise general awareness of data protection obligations in the Egyptian market.
