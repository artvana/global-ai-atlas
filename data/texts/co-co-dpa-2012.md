## Summary and Analysis

### Purpose and Scope

Colombia's Law 1581 of 2012, enacted as an Ley Estatutaria (a category of legislation requiring supermajority congressional approval and mandatory constitutional court review, reflecting its status as fundamental rights legislation), is the cornerstone of Colombia's data protection framework. It was promulgated on 17 October 2012 and regulated in detail by Decree 1377 of 2013 (later incorporated into Decree 1074 of 2015 as the Sole Regulatory Decree for Commerce). The Law is grounded in the constitutional right of habeas data recognized in Article 15 of the 1991 Colombian Constitution, which guarantees every person the right to know, update, and rectify information gathered about them in databases or files.

The Law applies to the processing of personal data carried out in Colombian territory or where the data controller is established in Colombia. It applies equally to processing carried out in third countries where the data controller has engaged a national processor. The Law covers personal data of natural persons in both the public and private sectors. However, it expressly excludes certain categories of processing: personal data incorporated in journalistic publications and other media protected by freedom of information; databases maintained for strictly personal or domestic purposes; databases maintained for national security, defense, and counterterrorism purposes; databases of credit intelligence regulated by Law 1266 of 2008 (the Habeas Data Law for financial data); and statistical databases maintained by the national statistical authority (DANE) under confidentiality.

Colombia's Law 1581 was found constitutionally sound by the Constitutional Court (Sentence C-748 of 2011 on the bill that became the Law). The Law has been supplemented over the years by the Superintendency of Industry and Commerce (SIC) through circulars and guidance. Law 1266 of 2008 continues to govern specifically the processing of financial and credit data, making Colombia one of the few jurisdictions with separate statutory regimes for general and financial personal data processing.

### Key Definitions

**Personal data** (dato personal) means any information linked or that can be associated with one or more determined or determinable natural persons.

**Sensitive data** (datos sensibles) is defined explicitly and includes data that may affect the intimacy of the person or whose improper use could generate discrimination: racial or ethnic origin; political orientation; religious or philosophical convictions; membership in trade unions, social organizations, human rights organizations; data relating to health, sexual life, or biometric data.

**Semi-private data** (datos semiprivados) refers to data that is not purely intimate but whose disclosure is not entirely free, such as financial and credit data and professional conduct data.

**Public data** (datos públicos) means data qualified as such by law or by its nature, including civil status, profession, and data contained in public registers.

**Data owner** (titular) is the natural person whose personal data is subject to processing.

**Data controller** (responsable del tratamiento) is the natural or legal person, public or private, that decides on the database and/or the processing of the data.

**Data processor** (encargado del tratamiento) is the natural or legal person, public or private, that performs data processing on behalf of the controller.

### Lawful Bases and Core Processing Principles

**Lawful Basis.** The central pillar of Law 1581 is consent. The Law requires that personal data may only be processed with the prior, express, and informed authorization of the data owner. Authorization may be obtained in writing, orally (provided it can be proven), or through unambiguous conduct. Exceptions to the consent requirement are limited and include: processing required by law; processing necessary for the performance of a contract where the owner is a party or beneficiary; processing for the protection of the data owner's vital interests; processing for historical, statistical, or scientific purposes where data is anonymized; and processing related to data that is of a public nature.

**Prior Authorization and Evidence.** The Law places particular emphasis on the controller's obligation to obtain and retain evidence of prior authorization. Controllers must be able to demonstrate that they obtained the data owner's authorization before commencing processing. Authorization provided electronically must meet standards ensuring its genuineness and preserving the record.

**Data Quality Principles.** Processing of personal data must comply with the following principles: truthfulness (veracidad) — data must be accurate, complete, and verifiable; purpose (finalidad) — processing must have a legitimate, specific purpose; freedom (libertad) — processing requires authorization; transparency (transparencia) — owners have the right to know about their data processing; restricted access (acceso y circulación restringida) — access to data must be controlled and lawful; security — controllers must adopt technical and administrative measures; confidentiality — persons involved in processing are bound to confidentiality even after the relationship ends.

**Sensitive Data Protections.** Processing of sensitive data is prohibited except: where explicitly authorized by law; for medical or public health purposes; for epidemiological research or national security reasons; where the data subject gives explicit authorization; and for the activities of foundations, NGOs, and non-profit entities related to political parties, unions, or religious organizations with respect to their own members. Even where sensitive data processing is permitted, additional protections apply: data subjects must be informed of the optional nature of providing sensitive data and the specific purpose; and data subjects may not be conditioned to provide sensitive data as a prerequisite for receipt of a product or service (unless reasonably justified).

**Databases.** Controllers must register databases containing personal data with the National Register of Databases maintained by the SIC. Registration requires disclosure of the purpose, categories of data, owners, data subjects, transfers, security measures, and the identity of the controller. The SIC may inspect registered databases as part of its oversight function.

### Rights of Data Owners

Law 1581 establishes a robust set of rights for data owners, grounded in the constitutional habeas data guarantee:

**Right to Access.** Data owners have the right to obtain from controllers and processors at any time and without cost a copy of their personal data and information about its processing.

**Right to Update.** Data owners may request that incomplete or outdated data be completed or updated.

**Right to Rectification.** Data owners may request correction of inaccurate, erroneous, or false data.

**Right to Deletion (Suppression).** Data owners may request the deletion of personal data that: is not necessary for the original purpose; was obtained in violation of the Law; or whose continued processing lacks a lawful basis. Deletion may be refused where the data owner has a legal obligation to maintain it, or where deletion would harm the legitimate rights of third parties.

**Right to Proof of Authorization.** Data owners may request evidence of the authorization given for processing of their personal data.

**Right to Complain.** Data owners may file complaints before the SIC against controllers or processors that violate the Law.

**Right to Information about Use.** Data owners have the right to be informed by the controller of the uses made of their personal data upon request.

**Right to Revoke Authorization.** Data owners may revoke their authorization for processing at any time. Where revocation is exercised, the controller must cease processing, unless continued processing is required by legal mandate, by contract, or by public interest.

Requests from data owners must be addressed within 15 business days (extendable by 8 business days). Where the request cannot be granted, the controller must explain the reasons for denial, advising the owner of the right to file a complaint with the SIC.

### Sensitive Data and Special Categories

Law 1581 imposes special obligations when processing sensitive data. Beyond the prohibition on conditioning goods and services on the provision of sensitive data, controllers must: inform data owners that sensitive data is optional to provide; specify clearly for what purpose the sensitive data will be used; and implement enhanced security measures for sensitive data. The processing of sensitive data pertaining to children and adolescents is subject to the best interests of the child principle and requires authorization from their legal representative.

### Cross-Border Transfer Rules

Law 1581 regulates international transfers of personal data by establishing a prior authorization regime. Controllers may transfer personal data to recipients in foreign countries only where:

- The recipient country provides a level of data protection equivalent to Colombia's;
- The data owner has given express authorization;
- The transfer is necessary for the protection of the data owner's vital interests where the owner is physically or legally incapacitated;
- The transfer is lawfully required for the recognition, exercise, or defense of a right before a judicial authority; or
- The transfer forms part of an international treaty or convention ratified by Colombia.

The SIC maintains and publishes a list of countries considered to provide adequate protection. Transfers to non-adequate countries may proceed under data transfer agreements (contratos de transmisión) that impose obligations on the recipient equivalent to those of the Law. The SIC must be notified of international data transmission agreements.

Importantly, Colombia distinguishes between **transmission** (transferring data to processors in other countries) and **transfer** (transferring data to new controllers in other countries). Both activities are regulated, but transmission to processors is subject to a contractual framework, while transfer to new controllers is subject to the adequacy or consent regime.

### Automated Decision-Making

Law 1581 of 2012 does not contain provisions specifically addressing automated decision-making or profiling in the manner of GDPR Article 22 or similar modern frameworks. The Law was enacted before the widespread deployment of AI and algorithmic decision-making in Colombia. However, the Law's general principles — particularly the purpose limitation, transparency, and data quality principles — have been applied by the SIC in its enforcement actions to address certain algorithmic processing practices.

The SIC has issued guidance indicating that credit scoring models, behavioral profiling for marketing, and risk classification systems used by insurance companies must comply with the general principles of Law 1581 with respect to the underlying personal data used. Controllers using automated systems for processing personal data must obtain appropriate authorization, must provide transparent information about the nature of the automated processing, and must provide data owners with mechanisms to exercise their rights including rectification.

Colombia's more recent regulatory developments — including the AI Ethics guidelines issued by MinTIC in 2023 and the various sectoral AI frameworks under development — are expected to supplement Law 1581's general framework with more specific automated decision-making protections. The government has indicated that comprehensive AI legislation is under consideration that would, inter alia, explicitly address algorithmic decision-making in employment, credit, and public services.

### Enforcement and Penalties

The **Superintendency of Industry and Commerce (SIC)** is Colombia's primary data protection enforcement authority. The SIC's Delegatura for Personal Data Protection handles complaints, investigations, and sanctions. The SIC has authority to: impose fines on controllers and processors; order the temporary suspension of processing operations; direct the suspension or shutdown of databases; and require compliance with specific protective measures.

The Law authorizes the SIC to impose fines of up to **2,000 legal minimum monthly wages** (salarios mínimos mensuales legales vigentes — SMMLV), which as of 2025 equates to approximately COP 3.2 billion (approximately USD 800,000). In addition, daily fines may be imposed for non-compliance with SIC orders, and repeated or aggravated violations may result in escalating sanctions.

The Law also authorizes suspension of data processing operations for up to six months as a precautionary measure during investigation, and permanent suspension of databases in cases of serious violations. Directors and officers of entities may be held personally liable in cases of willful misconduct.

Data owners may also bring civil claims for damages arising from violations of their habeas data rights, claiming both material and moral damages. The Law does not create a private cause of action for breaches of its provisions per se, but data owners may use the general civil liability framework in conjunction with constitutional tutela (injunctive) actions to seek redress.

The SIC has a well-established enforcement record and has imposed fines on major financial institutions, telecommunications companies, and marketing firms for violations including processing without authorization, inadequate security measures, excessive retention of data, and non-registration of databases. Colombia's data protection enforcement environment is considered one of the more active in Latin America, and the SIC regularly publishes guidance and enforcement decisions to assist regulated entities in understanding their compliance obligations.
