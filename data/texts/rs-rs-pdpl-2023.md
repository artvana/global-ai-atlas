## Summary and Analysis

### Purpose and Scope

Serbia's Law on Personal Data Protection (Zakon o zaštiti podataka o ličnosti), published as Official Gazette of the Republic of Serbia No. 87/2018 and entering into force on 21 August 2019, is Serbia's primary legislation governing the protection of personal data. The Law was adopted as a deliberate effort to harmonize Serbia's legal framework with the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (Regulation 2016/679), reflecting Serbia's status as an EU accession candidate. The degree of harmonization with the GDPR is exceptionally high — the Law essentially transposes the GDPR into Serbian national law, using equivalent provisions on lawful processing, data subject rights, controller and processor obligations, transfers, and enforcement.

The file identifier rs-rs-pdpl-2023 reflects the Law's continued application and its role in governing AI and automated processing as interpreted and applied in 2023, including in light of updated guidance from the Commissioner for Information of Public Importance and Personal Data Protection (the "Commissioner"). While the core statute dates to 2018 (in force 2019), the Law's provisions on automated decision-making — closely mirroring GDPR Article 22 — have become increasingly significant as AI systems have proliferated in Serbian commercial, financial, and public sector contexts.

The Law applies to: all processing of personal data carried out entirely or partly by automated means; and to processing of personal data that forms or is intended to form part of a filing system, where the processing is carried out in Serbia; where the controller is established in Serbia; or where the data subject is located in Serbia and the controller offers goods or services to persons in Serbia or monitors their behavior. The Law applies to public authorities and private entities equally. Specific exemptions cover processing for national security, intelligence, and defense purposes under separate enabling legislation, and processing for purely personal or household purposes.

The **Commissioner for Information of Public Importance and Personal Data Protection** serves as Serbia's supervisory authority. The Commissioner is an independent institution established by law, with powers to investigate complaints, conduct audits, issue binding decisions, and impose fines. The Commissioner cooperates with EU data protection authorities as part of Serbia's EU accession process, and the Commissioner's decisions and guidance have been broadly consistent with EDPB (European Data Protection Board) interpretations.

### Key Definitions

The Law's definitions track the GDPR very closely:

**Personal data** means any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person; an identifiable natural person is one who can be identified, directly or indirectly, in particular by reference to an identifier such as a name, identification number, location data, an online identifier, or to one or more factors specific to their physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity.

**Sensitive data** (special categories of personal data) includes data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, trade union membership, genetic data, biometric data processed for unique identification, health data, and data concerning sex life or sexual orientation.

**Controller** means any natural or legal person, public authority, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of processing personal data.

**Processor** means any natural or legal person, public authority, or body that processes personal data on behalf of the controller.

**Data subject** means the natural person to whom the personal data relates.

**Profiling** means any form of automated processing of personal data consisting of the use of personal data to evaluate certain personal aspects relating to a natural person, in particular to analyze or predict aspects concerning that person's performance at work, economic situation, health, personal preferences, interests, reliability, behavior, location, or movements.

**Consent** must be freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous; it must be demonstrated through a clear affirmative act.

### Principles and Core Obligations

The Law establishes data protection principles identical to those of the GDPR: lawfulness, fairness, and transparency; purpose limitation; data minimization; accuracy; storage limitation; integrity and confidentiality; and accountability. All processing must comply with these principles and controllers must be able to demonstrate compliance.

**Lawful Bases.** Processing is lawful only on the basis of: the data subject's consent; performance of a contract; compliance with a legal obligation; protection of vital interests; performance of a public interest task or exercise of official authority; or the legitimate interests of the controller or third parties, subject to a balancing test.

**Special Categories.** Processing of special categories of personal data is generally prohibited except: where the data subject gives explicit consent; for employment or social security purposes under applicable law; for vital interests; for activities of non-profit bodies with a political, philosophical, or religious aim; for manifestly public data; for legal proceedings; for substantial public interests; for health and social care purposes; for public health purposes; or for archiving, research, and statistical purposes.

**Transparency.** Controllers must provide data subjects with comprehensive information at the time of data collection or before first communication, covering all material aspects of processing including the existence of any automated decision-making and profiling.

**Security.** Controllers and processors must implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure security proportionate to the risk, taking into account the state of the art, implementation costs, and the nature, scope, context, and purposes of processing.

**Data Breach Notification.** Controllers must notify the Commissioner within 72 hours of becoming aware of a personal data breach that is likely to result in risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. High-risk breaches must also be communicated to affected data subjects without undue delay.

**Data Protection Impact Assessments.** Where processing is likely to result in high risk, controllers must carry out a DPIA prior to commencing processing. The Commissioner publishes lists of processing operations subject to mandatory DPIA requirements.

**Data Protection Officers.** DPOs are mandatory for: public authorities; controllers and processors whose core activities require large-scale, regular, and systematic monitoring of data subjects; and controllers and processors whose core activities consist of large-scale processing of special categories of data.

**Records of Processing Activities.** Controllers and processors must maintain records of processing activities.

### Automated Decision-Making — Article 38 (GDPR Article 22 Equivalent)

Article 38 of Serbia's Law on Personal Data Protection directly mirrors GDPR Article 22 and is the central provision governing AI and automated decision-making in Serbia's data protection framework.

**General Rule.** Data subjects have the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing — including profiling — which produces legal effects concerning them or similarly significantly affects them. This right applies to the full range of automated systems used in practice: credit scoring algorithms, recruitment AI tools, insurance pricing models, fraud detection systems that automatically flag or restrict accounts, social media content moderation systems, and any other AI or algorithmic tool that produces binding or materially impactful outputs without human intervention.

**Exceptions.** The prohibition on solely automated decisions does not apply where the decision:

(a) is necessary for entering into or performing a contract between the data subject and a controller;
(b) is authorized by applicable law providing suitable measures to safeguard the data subject's rights and legitimate interests; or
(c) is based on the data subject's explicit consent.

**Safeguards in Exceptional Cases.** Where a solely automated decision is made on the basis of an exception, the controller must:

- Implement suitable measures to safeguard the data subject's rights, freedoms, and legitimate interests;
- Guarantee, at minimum, the right of the data subject to obtain human intervention on the part of the controller;
- Ensure the data subject can express their point of view;
- Ensure the data subject can contest the decision.

**Special Categories.** Solely automated decisions based on special categories of personal data — including health data, genetic data, biometric data, or data revealing religious beliefs or political opinions — are prohibited unless the data subject has given explicit consent or the processing is necessary for substantial public interest reasons, subject to proportionate safeguards.

**Practical Application in Serbia.** The Commissioner for Information has issued guidance clarifying that Article 38 applies to decisions by banks, insurance companies, employers, and public authorities that use algorithmic tools to make or significantly influence decisions about individuals. The Commissioner has investigated cases where financial institutions used automated credit scoring systems without providing adequate information to customers or without implementing genuine human review procedures. The Commissioner's guidance emphasizes that "meaningful" human review requires the reviewer to have access to sufficient information and genuine authority to override the automated outcome — a nominal rubber-stamp review does not satisfy the standard.

### Transparency About Profiling

Where controllers process personal data through profiling — whether or not the profiling results in automated decisions — they must provide data subjects with information about the existence of the profiling, the categories of data used, the logic of the profiling, and the likely significance and impact on the data subject. This information must be provided as part of the general privacy notice required under the Law's transparency provisions. Where profiling produces significant effects, controllers must conduct a DPIA.

### Rights of Data Subjects

Serbia's Law provides data subjects with the full suite of GDPR-equivalent rights:

**Right of access.** Confirmation, copy, and information about processing.

**Right to rectification.** Correction of inaccurate or incomplete data.

**Right to erasure.** Deletion where data is no longer necessary, consent is withdrawn, an objection is upheld, or processing was unlawful.

**Right to restriction.** Restriction of processing while accuracy is contested, an objection is pending, or where the data subject needs data for legal claims.

**Right to portability.** Receipt of personal data in structured, machine-readable format.

**Right to object.** Objection to processing based on legitimate interests, for direct marketing, or for profiling.

**Rights in relation to automated decision-making.** Human review, expression of view, and contestation under Article 38.

**Right to withdraw consent.** Withdrawal at any time without prejudice to lawfulness of prior processing.

**Right to complain.** To the Commissioner, with the right to judicial review.

**Right to compensation.** Civil claims before Serbian courts for material and non-material damage.

Controllers must respond to data subject requests within 30 days, extendable in complex cases.

### Cross-Border Transfer Rules

The Law mirrors GDPR Chapter V on international data transfers. Transfers to third countries are permitted only where:

- The Commission has issued an adequacy decision for the recipient country (Serbia's list of adequate countries largely follows the EU adequacy list);
- The controller provides appropriate safeguards: standard contractual clauses, binding corporate rules, approved codes of conduct, or certification mechanisms;
- A specific derogation applies: explicit consent, contract necessity, vital interests, legal claims, or compelling public interest.

As an EU accession candidate, Serbia is subject to significant pressure to maintain data protection standards equivalent to those of the GDPR, and the Commissioner actively monitors compliance with transfer rules.

### Enforcement and Penalties

The Commissioner for Information of Public Importance and Personal Data Protection is Serbia's enforcement authority. The Commissioner may: conduct investigations on complaint or ex officio; issue warnings and recommendations; order compliance; impose fines; and suspend processing.

Administrative fines under the Law mirror the GDPR's two-tier structure:

**Lower tier (up to RSD 2,000,000 / approximately EUR 17,000 for legal persons, RSD 100,000 for natural persons):** For violations of general obligations including security, record-keeping, DPO appointment, and breach notification.

**Higher tier (up to RSD 5,000,000 / approximately EUR 42,000 for legal persons):** For violations of the fundamental principles of the Law, unlawful processing, failure to respect data subject rights, and unauthorized international transfers.

For large enterprises, higher fines may be calculated as a percentage of annual turnover, consistent with the GDPR model. The Commissioner applies a proportionality analysis in determining fines, taking into account the nature, gravity, and duration of the violation; the number of affected data subjects; whether the violation was intentional or negligent; the categories of personal data involved; and the measures taken to mitigate harm.

Data subjects may file complaints with the Commissioner and may also bring civil actions in Serbian courts for damages. Administrative decisions of the Commissioner are subject to judicial review before the Administrative Court.

Serbia's enforcement record has been developing progressively since the Law's entry into force in 2019, with the Commissioner increasingly active in investigations and enforcement actions, particularly in the financial services, telecommunications, and public administration sectors. The Commissioner's growing capacity and engagement with EU data protection authorities as part of the accession process signal continued strengthening of Serbia's data protection enforcement environment.
