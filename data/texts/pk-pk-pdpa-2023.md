## Summary and Analysis

### Purpose and Scope

Pakistan's Personal Data Protection Act 2023 (PDPA) is Pakistan's first comprehensive, standalone statute governing the collection, processing, storage, and use of personal data. Enacted by the National Assembly and receiving presidential assent on 25 October 2023, the PDPA reflects Pakistan's recognition that the rapid digitization of its economy, the growth of e-commerce and fintech, the deployment of biometric national identity systems by NADRA, and the country's significant IT and business process outsourcing sector all require a modern, internationally compatible data protection framework. The PDPA draws on the GDPR as a primary reference point, while also incorporating elements from ASEAN frameworks and the data protection laws of regional neighbors.

The PDPA applies to: any natural or legal person (public or private) that processes personal data in Pakistan; any controller or processor established in Pakistan regardless of where processing occurs; and any controller established outside Pakistan that processes personal data of individuals in Pakistan in connection with offering goods or services to those individuals or monitoring their behavior in Pakistan. The Act covers both automated and non-automated processing of personal data.

Exemptions are provided for: processing for personal or household purposes with no commercial connection; processing by competent authorities for law enforcement, national security, or intelligence purposes under applicable enabling legislation; processing for statistical or academic research under appropriate anonymization or aggregation; and journalistic, artistic, and literary activities where the public interest in freedom of expression overrides the privacy interest.

The **Pakistan Data Protection Authority (PDPA Authority)** — distinct from the statute's abbreviation — is established as an independent regulatory body under the Ministry of Information Technology and Telecommunication. The PDPA Authority is headed by a Chairperson and board of members appointed by the Federal Government. Its functions include issuing regulations and guidance, maintaining the national register of controllers, receiving and adjudicating complaints, conducting audits and inspections, and imposing administrative penalties.

### Key Definitions

**Personal data** means any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person. Identifiability includes indirect identification through combining data elements. The definition encompasses digital identifiers such as email addresses, IP addresses, device identifiers, and online behavioral data.

**Sensitive personal data** is defined comprehensively as data concerning: health and medical history; biometric data used for unique identification; genetic data; racial or ethnic origin; political opinions; religious beliefs; criminal records; financial account information; sexual orientation; and any other category designated by the PDPA Authority by regulation. Sensitive personal data is subject to heightened consent and security requirements.

**Data controller** (or simply "controller") means the natural or legal person, public authority, or body that, alone or jointly, determines the purpose and means of processing personal data.

**Data processor** means any person or body processing personal data on behalf of and under the instruction of a controller.

**Data subject** means the natural person to whom the personal data relates.

**Consent** must be free, specific, informed, unconditional, and unambiguous. Consent obtained through coercion, deception, or as a bundled condition of access to services (where personal data use is not necessary for the service) does not constitute valid consent. Consent must be demonstrably obtained and documented.

**Significant data controller** means any data controller designated by the PDPA Authority based on criteria such as processing volume, the sensitivity of data processed, the scale of profiling activities, potential impact on national security, or the relevance of processing to public welfare. Significant data controllers are subject to enhanced obligations including mandatory DPOs, annual compliance audits, and stricter cross-border transfer controls.

### Core Obligations for Data Controllers and Processors

**Lawful Processing Grounds.** Controllers must process personal data on the basis of at least one of the following: consent of the data subject; performance of a contract; compliance with a legal obligation; protection of vital interests; performance of a public interest task; or legitimate interests of the controller, balanced against the data subject's rights and interests. Sensitive personal data requires explicit consent except in narrowly defined alternative circumstances including public health emergencies, employment law, and vital interests.

**Transparency — Privacy Policy.** Controllers must publish a privacy policy accessible to data subjects explaining: the controller's identity; the purposes and legal bases for processing; the categories of data collected; recipients; international transfers and safeguards; retention periods; the data subject's rights; whether automated decision-making or profiling is conducted; and contact details for complaints. The privacy policy must be provided to data subjects at or before the time of data collection.

**Purpose Limitation and Data Minimization.** Processing must be confined to specified purposes, and data collected must be the minimum necessary for those purposes.

**Accuracy and Storage Limitation.** Controllers must take reasonable steps to ensure data accuracy and must not retain data beyond the period necessary for the stated purpose.

**Security Obligations.** Controllers and processors must implement appropriate technical and organizational security measures proportionate to the nature of the data and the risks involved. Security measures must be documented and subject to periodic review.

**Data Breach Notification.** Controllers must notify the PDPA Authority of personal data breaches that pose a risk to data subjects' rights and interests without undue delay, and no later than **72 hours** after becoming aware of the breach. High-risk breaches must also be communicated to affected data subjects without undue delay. Processors must notify the controller as soon as they become aware of a breach involving the controller's data.

**Data Protection Impact Assessments.** High-risk processing — including large-scale processing of sensitive personal data, systematic profiling, processing of children's data, biometric processing, and processing using new technologies — requires a DPIA before commencement. Where a DPIA reveals high residual risk, the controller must consult the PDPA Authority.

**Data Protection Officer.** Significant data controllers and processors engaged in large-scale processing of sensitive personal data or large-scale systematic monitoring must appoint a DPO. The DPO must have expertise in data protection law and must operate independently.

**Records of Processing Activities.** Controllers and processors must maintain detailed processing records documenting purposes, categories, recipients, transfers, retention periods, and security measures. Records must be made available to the PDPA Authority on request.

**Children's Data.** Processing of personal data of children under 16 requires verifiable parental consent. Age verification and parental consent mechanisms must be appropriate to the context. Processing children's data for behavioral profiling, advertising, or commercial monitoring is prohibited without explicit parental authorization.

### Rights of Data Subjects

The PDPA grants data subjects the following rights, exercisable without charge in most circumstances:

**Right to information.** The right to receive a clear privacy notice before or at the time of data collection.

**Right of access.** The right to obtain confirmation of whether personal data is being processed and to receive a copy of that data with information about processing.

**Right to rectification.** The right to correct inaccurate or complete incomplete personal data.

**Right to erasure.** The right to have personal data deleted where no longer necessary, where consent is withdrawn, where an objection is upheld, or where processing was unlawful.

**Right to restriction.** The right to restrict processing while accuracy is contested or an objection is pending.

**Right to data portability.** The right to receive personal data in structured, machine-readable format and to have it transmitted to another controller where technically feasible and where processing is based on consent or contract.

**Right to object.** The right to object to processing based on legitimate interests and to processing for direct marketing and profiling.

**Right against automated decisions.** The right not to be subject to solely automated decisions with significant legal or similar effects, and to obtain human review.

**Right to withdraw consent.** The right to withdraw consent at any time without prejudice to lawfulness of prior processing.

**Right to complain.** The right to file a complaint with the PDPA Authority or seek compensation through civil proceedings.

Controllers must respond to data subject requests within **30 days**, extendable by a further **30 days** for complex cases. Refusals must be communicated with reasons.

### Automated Decision-Making Provisions

The PDPA includes dedicated provisions on automated decision-making and profiling. Data subjects have the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, that produces legal effects or that significantly and similarly affects them — such as decisions about employment, credit, insurance, housing, health services, or access to public benefits.

Exceptions apply where the automated decision is: necessary for performance of a contract; authorized by law; or based on the data subject's explicit consent. Where an exception applies, the controller must: inform the data subject that the decision was made by automated means; provide meaningful information about the logic, significance, and consequences of the automated processing; allow the data subject to express their point of view; and provide a mechanism for contesting the decision and obtaining human review.

Controllers using automated systems for profiling must conduct DPIAs and must implement measures to prevent discriminatory outcomes based on protected characteristics. The PDPA Authority may issue sector-specific guidance on automated processing.

### Cross-Border Transfer Rules

The PDPA establishes a comprehensive framework for international data transfers. Personal data may be transferred to a foreign country or territory only where:

- The PDPA Authority has issued an adequacy determination for the recipient jurisdiction;
- The controller has entered into a data transfer agreement with the foreign recipient incorporating obligations equivalent to those under the PDPA, using standard contractual clauses approved by the PDPA Authority or binding corporate rules;
- The transfer is covered by a recognized international agreement to which Pakistan is a party; or
- A specific derogation applies: explicit consent with full disclosure of risks; contract necessity; vital interests; legal claims; or compelling public interest.

Sensitive personal data is subject to stricter transfer controls. Certain categories may require specific authorization from the PDPA Authority before transfer, and the PDPA Authority may designate specific categories of data as subject to localization requirements based on national security or public interest considerations.

### Enforcement and Penalties

The PDPA Authority has broad investigatory and enforcement powers including the ability to conduct inspections, require production of documents, issue compliance orders, and suspend processing. Enforcement actions are subject to an internal appeals process within the PDPA Authority, and final decisions are subject to judicial review before the High Courts.

**Administrative penalties** are structured as follows:

- Minor violations (procedural failures, record-keeping deficiencies, inadequate privacy notices): fines up to **PKR 5,000,000** (approximately USD 17,000).
- Moderate violations (processing without lawful basis, failure to honor data subject rights, inadequate security measures): fines up to **PKR 15,000,000** (approximately USD 53,000).
- Serious violations (unlawful processing of sensitive personal data, unauthorized international transfers, obstruction of the PDPA Authority): fines up to **PKR 25,000,000** (approximately USD 89,000).
- For significant data controllers, the maximum fine for serious violations may be calculated as a percentage of annual turnover where this exceeds the fixed maximum.

**Criminal penalties** apply to willful violations: unauthorized acquisition, use, or disclosure of personal data for financial gain or to cause harm: imprisonment up to **3 years** and/or fines up to **PKR 5,000,000**. Corporate officers may be held personally liable where violations result from their willful act or negligence.

The PDPA Authority may also order: the suspension or cessation of unlawful processing; the deletion or destruction of unlawfully processed data; the notification of data subjects affected by violations; and publication of enforcement decisions.

In determining penalties, the PDPA Authority must consider: the nature and severity of the violation; the duration; the number of affected data subjects; the measures taken to mitigate harm; prior compliance history; the financial capacity of the controller; and whether the violation was willful.

### Implementation and Transition

The PDPA provides for a phased implementation period. Large enterprises and significant data controllers were required to achieve compliance within 12 months of the Act's commencement. Smaller enterprises benefit from extended transition periods. The PDPA Authority has been tasked with issuing regulations, codes of practice, and sector-specific guidance to assist entities in understanding and meeting their compliance obligations. The Authority has prioritized guidance for the financial services sector, telecommunications, e-commerce, and digital health platforms, which collectively account for the largest volumes of personal data processing in Pakistan's digital economy.
