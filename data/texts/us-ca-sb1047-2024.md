# Safe and Secure Innovation for Frontier Artificial Intelligence Models Act (California SB 1047)

## Overview

California Senate Bill 1047, introduced by Senator Scott Wiener on February 8, 2024, and passed by the California Legislature on August 28, 2024, would have created the most comprehensive frontier AI safety obligations of any US law to date. Governor Gavin Newsom vetoed the bill on September 29, 2024.

The bill targeted developers of large frontier AI models — those trained using computational power exceeding 10^26 floating-point operations (FLOPs) or costing more than $100 million to train. It would have required these developers to:

1. Implement a "safety and security program" before training any covered model, including written policies, procedures, and protections against potential harms.
2. Conduct pre-deployment safety testing to evaluate catastrophic risks, including risks of enabling mass-casualty weapons (biological, chemical, nuclear, radiological) or enabling critical infrastructure attacks.
3. Implement the ability to shut down or disable AI models ("kill switch" requirement) and protect model weights from unauthorized access.
4. Conduct annual third-party audits of their safety programs.
5. Retain records of safety testing and make them available to California's Attorney General.

The bill also would have created a new state agency, the **Frontier Model Division** within the California Department of Technology, staffed with technical experts to conduct oversight. A companion measure, AB 2013, addressed training data transparency and was signed into law; SB 1047 itself was not.

Enforcement would have been by the California Attorney General, with civil penalties up to 10% of the cost to train the covered model or up to 30% for knowing violations. A private right of action was removed in a late amendment.

## Why It Failed

Governor Newsom vetoed SB 1047 on September 29, 2024, the same day he signed several other AI bills (AB 2013, AB 2885, SB 942, SB 1120, AB 302). His veto message offered several grounds:

**1. Compute threshold is a poor proxy for risk.** Newsom argued that the bill's reliance on a fixed training-compute threshold (10^26 FLOPs) would both under- and over-regulate: it could capture relatively benign models trained at scale while missing highly dangerous smaller, specialized models. He cited testimony from AI researchers that the threshold was "arbitrary" and would quickly become outdated.

**2. Burden on California-based companies.** The Governor emphasized that California is home to the world's leading AI companies and that imposing compliance costs (safety programs, audits, liability exposure) uniquely on California-based developers would push companies to incorporate elsewhere or discourage frontier AI development in the state.

**3. Pre-emptive regulation of speculative harms.** Newsom expressed concern that the bill would impose binding obligations based on potential harms that "have not yet materialized" rather than documented evidence of actual risk from deployed systems.

**4. Chilling effect on open-source AI.** The bill's provisions — particularly the requirement to implement kill switches and protect model weights — would have been difficult or impossible to comply with for open-weight (open-source) models once released. Critics argued this effectively banned open-weight frontier models from California developers.

**5. California's AI Safety Institute should lead.** Newsom indicated he preferred to build California's regulatory capacity through the newly created California AI Safety Institute (via AB 302) before imposing binding developer obligations.

The veto generated significant national debate. Supporters (including AI safety researchers, the Center for AI Safety, and Senator Wiener) argued it was the strongest evidence that the tech industry could block meaningful AI regulation. Opponents of the bill (OpenAI, Google, Meta, Anthropic, and many AI researchers including Yann LeCun, Andrew Ng, and Geoffrey Hinton) supported the veto, arguing the bill was technically flawed and would harm innovation without meaningfully reducing risk.

## Key Provisions (as proposed)

### Covered Entities

- **Covered model**: A model trained with ≥10^26 integer or floating-point operations, or a fine-tuned model where fine-tuning itself used ≥10^23 operations if the fine-tuned model posed comparable risks.
- **Covered developer**: Any person who trained or caused the training of a covered model, with exceptions for contractors following another party's specifications.
- Entities with <$1 billion in annual revenue that did not develop the covered model were exempted as "small businesses."

### Safety and Security Program (Section 22603)

Developers were required to maintain a written safety and security program that included:
- Policies and procedures for evaluating whether a covered model poses an "unreasonable risk of causing or materially enabling a critical harm."
- Capability to fully shut down covered models (kill switch).
- Procedures to preserve model weights and prevent unauthorized access.
- Measures to implement the ability to add watermarks or other provenance indicators.
- Cybersecurity protections meeting NIST AI RMF and NIST CSF standards.

### Pre-Deployment Safety Testing

Before deploying or making available a covered model, developers must:
- Evaluate the model for "critical harm" risks, defined as:
  - Mass-casualty chemical, biological, radiological, or nuclear (CBRN) weapons creation.
  - Attacks on critical infrastructure (power grids, water systems, financial systems).
  - Creating cyberweapons capable of significant damage.
  - Actions by an AI acting with limited human oversight that cause $500M+ in damage.
- Document testing methodology and results.
- Retain records for at least three years.

### Third-Party Audits

- Annual independent audits of the safety and security program.
- Auditors must be certified by a list maintained by the Attorney General.
- Audit reports must be provided to the AG upon request.

### Frontier Model Division

- A new state agency within California Department of Technology.
- Authority to issue guidance on compliance, request documentation, investigate complaints.
- Required to develop and publish guidance on what constitutes adequate safety testing.

### Penalties

- Civil penalties up to 10% of the cost to train the covered model for non-willful violations.
- Civil penalties up to 30% of training cost for knowing or reckless violations.
- Attorney General enforcement only; private right of action removed from the final bill text.

### Key Definitions

- **Critical harm**: Specified categories above, plus any use of an AI system by a person acting outside the scope of authorization.
- **Hazardous capability evaluation**: Structured testing to assess whether a model can assist with mass-casualty weapons or critical infrastructure attacks.

### Safe Harbor

Developers who followed the Frontier Model Division's published guidance and tested their models in accordance with that guidance would receive a rebuttable presumption of compliance.

## Policy Significance

Despite never becoming law, SB 1047 was the most significant AI safety bill considered by any US legislature and shaped global policy discourse throughout 2024:

- It was the direct catalyst for Governor Newsom signing the more limited California SB 53 (2025), which retained the safety testing and incident reporting concepts while removing the shutdown requirements and reducing enforcement scope.
- It influenced the EU AI Act implementation debates around compute thresholds for GPAI models.
- It energized the AI safety advocacy community and demonstrated the political difficulty of passing frontier safety legislation even in the most AI-company-dense state.
- The bill's veto letter became a key reference point in debates about whether AI regulation should be evidence-based or precautionary.
- Senator Wiener explicitly modeled SB 53 (2025) as a response to the veto's specific objections, removing the audit requirements and kill switch mandate while retaining safety program disclosure and incident reporting.
