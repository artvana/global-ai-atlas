## Summary and Analysis

### Purpose and Scope

Rwanda's Law N° 058/2021 of 13 October 2021 Relating to the Protection of Personal Data and Privacy (the "Rwanda Data Protection Law" or "the Law") is Rwanda's comprehensive legislative framework governing the collection, processing, storage, transfer, and use of personal data. The Law came into effect on 15 October 2021 and was developed as part of Rwanda's National Information and Communications Infrastructure (NICI) policy and the country's Vision 2050 digital economy agenda, which emphasizes Rwanda's ambition to become a leading digital hub in Africa. The Law draws on GDPR principles and the African Union's Malabo Convention framework, and reflects Rwanda's well-established tradition of proactive, forward-looking legislation in support of its development objectives.

The Law applies to any processing of personal data by a natural or legal person — public or private — where: the processing is carried out in Rwanda; the controller or processor is established in Rwanda; or the processing relates to individuals who are in Rwanda even if the controller is established abroad. The Law covers both automated and non-automated processing of personal data in structured filing systems.

Exemptions apply for: processing for personal or household purposes; processing by competent authorities for prevention, investigation, detection, or prosecution of criminal offenses or execution of criminal penalties, including safeguarding against and preventing threats to public security; and processing for statistical or research purposes under appropriate safeguards.

The **National Cyber Security Authority (NCSA)** is designated as the primary regulatory authority responsible for overseeing implementation and enforcement of the Law, with specific data protection functions delegated to it. The NCSA operates under the Office of the President. It is empowered to issue binding regulations and guidance, maintain the register of data controllers, receive and investigate complaints, conduct audits, and impose administrative sanctions. The Law also envisages coordination with sector-specific regulators in telecommunications, financial services, and healthcare.

### Key Definitions

**Personal data** means any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person. A natural person is identifiable if they can be identified, directly or indirectly, by reference to an identifier such as a name, identification number, location data, or one or more factors specific to their physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social identity.

**Sensitive personal data** includes: data revealing racial or ethnic origin; political opinions; religious beliefs; trade union membership; genetic data; biometric data; health data; sex life or sexual orientation; financial data; criminal records; and any other category designated as sensitive by the NCSA.

**Data controller** means the natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of processing personal data.

**Data processor** means any natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or body that processes personal data on behalf of and under the instruction of a data controller.

**Data subject** means the natural person to whom the personal data relates.

**Consent** must be freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous, and demonstrated through a clear affirmative act. Silence, pre-ticked boxes, and inactivity do not constitute consent.

### Core Obligations for Data Controllers and Processors

**Lawful Basis for Processing.** Processing of personal data must be based on at least one of the following legal grounds: consent; contract performance; legal obligation; vital interests; public interest task; or the legitimate interests of the controller or third parties, provided those interests are not overridden by the data subject's fundamental rights.

For sensitive personal data, explicit consent is required, except in limited circumstances: employment and social protection law requirements; vital interests where the data subject is incapable of giving consent; non-profit body activities for members; manifestly public data; legal claims; substantial public interest; medical diagnosis and treatment; public health; and archiving, research, and statistics.

**Transparency and Privacy Notice.** Controllers must inform data subjects, prior to collection or at the time of collection, of: the controller's identity; the purposes and legal basis for processing; any intended recipients; international transfers and safeguards; retention periods; and the data subject's rights. Where data is not collected directly from the data subject, the controller must provide this information within a reasonable time or before the first communication with the data subject.

**Purpose Limitation and Data Minimization.** Data must be collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes and must not be processed beyond those purposes. Data collected must be limited to what is necessary.

**Accuracy and Storage Limitation.** Controllers must take reasonable steps to ensure accuracy and must not retain data beyond the period necessary for the stated purpose.

**Security Obligations.** Controllers and processors must implement appropriate technical and organizational security measures proportionate to the risk. Processors must be engaged under binding written agreements imposing equivalent security obligations.

**Data Breach Notification.** Controllers must notify the NCSA of personal data breaches likely to result in risk to data subjects' rights and freedoms without undue delay, and within 72 hours of becoming aware of the breach where feasible. High-risk breaches must also be communicated to affected data subjects. The notification must describe the nature of the breach, the categories and approximate number of data subjects and records affected, the likely consequences, and the measures taken to address the breach.

**Data Protection Impact Assessments.** Where processing is likely to result in high risk — including systematic monitoring of public areas, large-scale processing of sensitive personal data, or processing using new technologies — controllers must conduct a DPIA prior to commencing processing. Where a DPIA reveals high residual risk, the controller must consult with the NCSA before proceeding.

**Data Protection Officer.** Public authorities, and controllers and processors whose core activities consist of large-scale regular and systematic monitoring of data subjects or large-scale processing of sensitive personal data, must appoint a DPO. The DPO must be registered with the NCSA and must have appropriate expertise in data protection.

**Records of Processing.** Controllers and processors must maintain records of their processing activities documenting purposes, categories, recipients, transfers, security measures, and retention periods.

**Children's Data.** Processing the personal data of children requires parental or guardian consent. Processing for behavioral profiling or commercial advertising of children is subject to heightened restrictions.

### Rights of Data Subjects

The Law grants data subjects the following rights:

**Right to be informed.** The right to receive comprehensive information about processing before or at the time of collection.

**Right of access.** The right to obtain confirmation of whether personal data is being processed and, if so, to receive a copy of that data along with information about the processing.

**Right to rectification.** The right to have inaccurate or incomplete personal data corrected.

**Right to erasure.** The right to have personal data deleted where it is no longer necessary, consent is withdrawn, an objection is upheld, or processing was unlawful.

**Right to restriction.** The right to restrict processing in specified circumstances, including while accuracy is contested or an objection is pending.

**Right to data portability.** The right to receive personal data in a structured, commonly used, machine-readable format and to transmit it to another controller where processing is based on consent or contract and carried out by automated means.

**Right to object.** The right to object to processing based on legitimate interests, including profiling for those purposes, and to processing for direct marketing.

**Rights in relation to automated decisions.** The right not to be subject to solely automated decisions with significant effects, and to request human review.

**Right to withdraw consent.** The right to withdraw consent at any time without prejudice to lawfulness of prior processing.

**Right to complain.** The right to file a complaint with the NCSA.

**Right to judicial remedy.** The right to seek compensation through the courts for violations of rights under the Law.

Controllers must respond to data subject requests within 30 days, extendable by a further 30 days for complex requests. Requests may be refused on limited grounds, with reasons provided to the data subject.

### Cross-Border Transfer Rules

The Law establishes a framework for international data transfers that conditions transfers on the provision of adequate protection in the recipient jurisdiction. Personal data may be transferred to a foreign country or international organization only where:

- The NCSA has determined that the recipient country provides an adequate level of data protection;
- The controller has implemented appropriate safeguards: standard contractual clauses approved by the NCSA, binding corporate rules, or approved codes of conduct; or
- A specific derogation applies: explicit consent with full disclosure of risks; contract necessity; vital interests; legal claims; or compelling public interest.

For sensitive personal data, transfers are subject to stricter controls and may require NCSA authorization. The NCSA maintains and publishes a list of countries considered adequate.

Rwanda's regional integration commitments — including under the East African Community (EAC) and the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) — are reflected in the Law's provisions allowing for simplified transfer procedures between countries party to relevant regional agreements that provide equivalent data protection.

### Automated Processing Provisions

The Law includes provisions addressing automated decision-making and profiling consistent with the GDPR Article 22 model. Data subjects have the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing — including profiling — that produces legal effects or similarly significantly affects them.

Exceptions apply where the automated decision: is necessary for entering into or performing a contract; is authorized by Rwandan law providing suitable safeguards; or is based on the data subject's explicit consent. Where an exception applies, the controller must: implement appropriate safeguards for the data subject's rights; guarantee the data subject's right to obtain human intervention; ensure the data subject can express their point of view; and allow the data subject to contest the decision.

Controllers engaged in profiling or automated decision-making must conduct DPIAs and must implement measures to prevent discriminatory or erroneous outcomes. The NCSA may issue guidance on the application of the automated processing provisions to specific sectors.

Rwanda's emerging digital financial services sector — including mobile money platforms and digital credit services — makes the automated processing provisions particularly relevant, as these services increasingly use algorithmic scoring to make lending and account access decisions affecting large numbers of Rwandan residents.

### Enforcement and Penalties

The NCSA has broad investigatory and enforcement powers, including the ability to conduct inspections, require production of information, issue compliance orders, suspend processing, and impose administrative fines.

**Administrative penalties** for violations of the Law are tiered by severity:

- **Minor violations** (procedural failures, inadequate record-keeping, minor transparency deficiencies): written warnings and fines of up to RWF 10,000,000 (approximately USD 9,000).
- **Serious violations** (processing without lawful basis, failing to honor data subject rights, inadequate security measures, failure to notify breaches): fines up to RWF 50,000,000 (approximately USD 45,000).
- **Very serious violations** (unlawful processing of sensitive personal data, unauthorized international transfers, obstruction of the NCSA): fines up to RWF 100,000,000 (approximately USD 90,000) or higher amounts calculated by reference to the annual revenue of the violating entity.

For repeat violations or violations causing significant harm, the NCSA may additionally order suspension of processing operations, public censure, and referral to the Rwandan National Public Prosecution Authority for criminal proceedings.

**Criminal penalties** may apply for willful unauthorized collection, use, or disclosure of personal data: imprisonment of up to two years and/or fines. Corporate officers may be held personally liable where violations result from their negligence or willful conduct.

In determining penalties, the NCSA considers: the nature, gravity, and duration of the violation; the number of affected data subjects; the measures taken to mitigate harm; the history of compliance; and the financial capacity of the controller.

### Significance and Regional Context

Rwanda's Data Protection Law is part of a broader wave of African data protection legislation and reflects Rwanda's commitment to aligning its legal framework with international standards in support of its digital economy ambitions. The Law positions Rwanda to participate in international data flows with partner countries that require adequate data protection as a condition of transfer, including EU member states. Rwanda's National Cyber Security Authority has been active in developing implementation capacity, and the country's high mobile internet penetration and dynamic digital economy make robust enforcement increasingly important. The Law serves as a model for other EAC member states developing their own data protection frameworks.
