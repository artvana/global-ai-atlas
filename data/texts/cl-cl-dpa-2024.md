## Summary and Analysis

### Purpose and Scope

Ley 21.719, published in the Diario Oficial on 13 December 2024, represents the most comprehensive reform of Chile's personal data protection framework since the enactment of Law 19.628 in 1999. The 2024 Law substantially amends Law 19.628 while introducing new institutional architecture, expanded individual rights, an AI-specific automated decision-making chapter, and a meaningful enforcement regime. It positions Chile as the first Latin American country with a fully modernized, GDPR-compatible data protection law, making Chile an attractive hub for data-processing enterprises seeking a robust and internationally recognized legal framework in the region.

The Law applies to the processing of personal data of natural persons by any entity — whether public or private, incorporated or unincorporated, resident or non-resident — that processes personal data in connection with activities carried out in Chilean territory or that processes personal data of persons located in Chile, regardless of the controller's place of establishment. The territoriality provisions are explicitly extraterritorial in scope, following the GDPR model. The Law covers both automated and non-automated processing of personal data where personal data is contained in or intended to be part of a structured filing system.

Limited exemptions apply for: processing carried out by natural persons in the course of purely personal or household activities; processing for exclusively journalistic, artistic, or literary purposes where this serves the public interest; and processing for national security and defense purposes subject to separate sectoral legislation.

The Law creates the **Agencia de Protección de Datos Personales (APDP)** — Chile's first dedicated, independent data protection authority — as a public law entity endowed with technical, functional, and financial autonomy. The APDP is empowered to issue binding regulations, guidance, and codes of practice; conduct investigations and audits; receive and adjudicate complaints; impose administrative sanctions; and participate in international data protection cooperation.

### Key Definitions

**Personal data** means any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person. Identifiability is assessed broadly and includes indirect identification through combining multiple data elements.

**Sensitive personal data** (datos sensibles) encompasses data revealing racial or ethnic origin; political opinions; religious or philosophical beliefs; trade union membership; genetic data; biometric data for unique identification; health data; data concerning sex life or sexual orientation; financial data revealing economic or patrimonial situations; migration status; and criminal records or charges.

**Data controller** (responsable del tratamiento) is the natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body that alone or jointly with others determines the purposes and means of processing personal data.

**Data processor** (mandatario o encargado del tratamiento) is the natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body that processes personal data on behalf of and under the instructions of the controller.

**Data subject** (titular de los datos) is the natural person to whom the personal data relates.

**Consent** must be freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous. Pre-ticked boxes, silence, and inactivity do not constitute valid consent. Consent may be withdrawn at any time.

**Profiling** means any form of automated processing of personal data consisting of the use of personal data to evaluate certain personal aspects relating to a natural person, in particular to analyze or predict aspects relating to performance at work, economic situation, health, personal preferences, interests, reliability, behavior, location, or movements.

### Lawful Bases for Processing and Core Obligations

**Lawful Bases.** The Law establishes an exhaustive list of lawful bases for processing personal data: consent; performance of a legal obligation; performance of a contract; protection of vital interests; public interest task; and legitimate interests of the controller or third parties, subject to balancing with the data subject's rights. For sensitive personal data, only explicit consent, legal obligation, or vital interests suffice in most cases, with narrow additional grounds for health and legal purposes.

**Purpose Limitation and Data Minimization.** Controllers must collect personal data for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes and must not process data beyond those purposes. Processing must be limited to what is strictly necessary for the stated purpose.

**Accuracy, Storage Limitation, and Security.** Personal data must be accurate and kept up to date. Controllers must implement appropriate technical and organizational security measures proportionate to the nature of the data and the risks involved. Data must not be retained beyond the period necessary for the original purpose.

**Records of Processing Activities.** Controllers and processors must maintain records of processing activities documenting, inter alia, the name and contact details of the controller, the purposes of processing, categories of data subjects and personal data, recipients of personal data, international transfers, and retention periods.

**Data Protection by Design and Default.** The Law requires controllers to integrate data protection principles into the design of processing systems and to ensure that by default only personal data necessary for each specific purpose is processed.

**Privacy Notices.** At the time of data collection or as soon as practicable thereafter, controllers must provide data subjects with a comprehensive privacy notice covering: the identity and contact details of the controller and its representative; the purposes and legal basis for processing; any legitimate interests relied upon; recipients or categories of recipients; international transfers; retention periods; and the data subject's rights.

**Data Protection Officer (DPO).** Certain categories of controllers are required to appoint a DPO: public authorities; controllers whose core activities consist of large-scale, regular, and systematic monitoring of data subjects; and controllers whose core activities consist of large-scale processing of sensitive personal data. The DPO must have expert knowledge of data protection law and practice and must be afforded operational independence.

**Data Breach Notification.** In the event of a personal data breach that poses a risk to data subjects, controllers must notify the APDP within 72 hours of becoming aware of the breach. High-risk breaches must also be communicated to affected data subjects without undue delay.

**Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs).** Controllers must carry out a DPIA before processing that is likely to result in a high risk to data subjects, particularly processing involving large-scale use of new technologies, systematic profiling with significant effects, large-scale processing of sensitive data, or public area monitoring. Where a DPIA indicates high residual risk, the controller must consult with the APDP prior to processing.

### Rights of Data Subjects

Ley 21.719 provides data subjects with a comprehensive set of rights aligned with the GDPR:

**Right of Access.** Data subjects may request confirmation of whether their personal data is being processed and, if so, obtain access to that data and information about the processing.

**Right to Rectification.** Data subjects may request correction of inaccurate or incomplete personal data.

**Right to Erasure.** Data subjects may request deletion of their personal data where it is no longer necessary for the original purpose, consent has been withdrawn without another lawful basis, an objection has been upheld, or the data has been unlawfully processed.

**Right to Restriction.** Data subjects may request restricted processing while accuracy is contested, an objection is pending, or where erasure would deprive the data subject of personal data they need for legal claims.

**Right to Data Portability.** Where processing is based on consent or contract and carried out by automated means, data subjects may receive their data in structured, commonly used, machine-readable format and transmit it to another controller.

**Right to Object.** Data subjects may object to processing for legitimate interests (including profiling for those purposes) and to processing for direct marketing.

**Right to Human Review of Automated Decisions.** Data subjects have the right not to be subject to solely automated decisions that produce significant legal or similar effects, and to request human review of such decisions. This right is addressed in detail in the Law's AI-specific provisions described below.

Controllers must respond to data subject requests within 30 days, extendable by a further 30 days in complex cases. Requests may be refused only on specific grounds enumerated in the Law, and refusals must be notified with reasons.

### Cross-Border Transfer Rules

The Law prohibits the transfer of personal data to a third country unless: (1) the APDP has determined that the recipient country or territory provides an adequate level of protection; (2) the controller has provided appropriate safeguards, including standard contractual clauses, binding corporate rules, an approved code of conduct, or certification under an approved mechanism; or (3) a specific derogation applies, including explicit consent, contract necessity, vital interests, or compelling public interest.

The APDP is charged with developing and maintaining an adequacy list and with approving standard contractual clauses and other transfer mechanisms. For transfers involving sensitive personal data or large volumes of data, additional prior authorization may be required from the APDP.

### Automated Decision-Making and AI-Specific Provisions

One of Ley 21.719's most significant innovations is its dedicated chapter on automated decision-making, which goes beyond the baseline GDPR Article 22 framework to address specifically the risks posed by AI systems, algorithmic models, and machine learning tools.

**Right Against Solely Automated Decisions.** Data subjects have the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing — including profiling — that produces legal effects or significantly affects them in similarly material ways (such as affecting access to credit, employment, housing, health services, or public benefits). Controllers may make automated decisions in limited circumstances: where necessary for a contract; where authorized by law; or where the data subject has given explicit consent. In each permitted case, the controller must implement appropriate safeguards.

**Transparency of Algorithmic Logic.** Controllers using automated decision-making or profiling systems must provide data subjects with meaningful information about the logic of the automated processing, the significance of that processing for the data subject, and the likely consequences. This must be communicated in clear, plain language.

**Right to Human Review.** Where a controller makes an automated decision affecting a data subject pursuant to one of the permitted exceptions, the data subject must be able to obtain human review, express their point of view, and contest the decision. The human review must be conducted by a person with sufficient authority and information to genuinely reassess the automated outcome.

**Algorithmic Impact Assessments.** The Law introduces a specific obligation to conduct an **Algorithmic Impact Assessment (AIA)** before deploying any automated decision-making system or algorithmic profiling tool that is likely to produce significant effects on data subjects. The AIA must assess the purpose and logic of the system, the categories of data used, the accuracy and reliability of the model, the potential for discriminatory outputs, and the measures in place to mitigate identified risks. AIA results must be communicated to the APDP upon request.

**Children and Profiling.** Profiling of children and adolescents for commercial purposes or behavioral targeting is prohibited without explicit parental consent, and the Law imposes enhanced protections for minors' personal data across all processing contexts.

**Public Sector Automated Decisions.** Public authorities using automated systems to make decisions affecting citizens' legal rights or obligations must, in addition to complying with the above requirements, maintain publicly accessible registers of the automated systems in use, publish the criteria and logic applied, and subject those systems to periodic audits.

### Enforcement and Penalties

The APDP has broad investigatory and enforcement powers, including the ability to conduct audits, request information and documents, inspect premises, issue binding compliance orders, and impose administrative penalties. The APDP may also issue guidance and codes of practice that, while not legally binding in themselves, carry significant weight in enforcement proceedings.

The Law establishes a tiered penalty structure:

**Minor infractions** (e.g., deficiencies in record-keeping, failure to publish a privacy policy, minor procedural failures): fines up to 5,000 Monthly Tax Units (UTM), equivalent to approximately USD 375,000 at 2024 rates.

**Serious infractions** (e.g., processing personal data without a lawful basis, failing to honor data subject rights, failing to notify a data breach): fines up to 10,000 UTM (approximately USD 750,000).

**Very serious infractions** (e.g., processing sensitive personal data unlawfully, unauthorized international data transfers, obstructing the APDP): fines up to 20,000 UTM (approximately USD 1.5 million).

For entities with significant turnover, penalties can be calculated as a percentage of global annual turnover, capped at 4% for the most serious violations, aligning the enforcement regime with the GDPR model.

Data subjects also have the right to seek compensation through civil proceedings for material and non-material damages caused by violations of the Law.

### Entry into Force and Transition

Ley 21.719 has a phased entry into force. The majority of provisions apply 24 months after publication, i.e., from approximately December 2026. The APDP was established and began operating earlier, in 2025. During the transition period, controllers are expected to prepare compliance programs, appoint DPOs where required, update their privacy notices and data processing agreements, and conduct DPIAs and AIAs for existing high-risk processing activities. The APDP has committed to issuing detailed guidance on each major area of the Law during the transition period.
