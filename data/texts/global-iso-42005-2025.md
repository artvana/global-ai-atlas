SCOPE

ISO/IEC 42005:2025 provides guidance for organizations performing AI system impact assessments for individuals and societies that can be affected by an AI system and its foreseeable applications. The document is applicable to any organization, regardless of size, type and nature, that is developing, providing, or using AI systems.

The standard includes considerations for:
- How and when to perform impact assessments
- At what stages of the AI system life cycle assessments should be conducted
- Guidance for AI system impact assessment documentation
- How the AI system impact assessment process can be integrated into an organization's AI risk management and AI management system

This is the first international standard dedicated specifically to AI system impact assessment.

CORE PRINCIPLES

The standard rests on three foundational pillars:

Fairness: Goes beyond non-discrimination to encompass equitable treatment and just outcomes. Acknowledges multiple fairness definitions (individual, group, procedural, distributive) requiring explicit organizational choices.

Safety: Encompasses immediate risks (system failures) and longer-term systemic impacts. Applies safety engineering principles adapted for AI-specific challenges like distributional shift and emergent behaviors.

Human-Centred Design: Ensures AI systems maintain human agency and dignity. Emphasizes human values and context in system design and evaluation.

SCOPE OF ASSESSMENT — FIVE DIMENSIONS

The standard requires evaluating impacts across five dimensions:
1. Social: Employment patterns, social cohesion, community dynamics
2. Economic: Productivity effects, market competition, labor displacement, inequality
3. Environmental: Carbon footprint, resource consumption, sustainability
4. Ethical: Fairness, transparency, accountability, human autonomy
5. Governance: Decision accountability and processes

The standard explicitly connects to UN Sustainable Development Goals 5, 8, 9, 10, and 12.

KEY ASSESSMENT COMPONENTS

1. Impact Identification: Systematic discovery of intended and unintended consequences across stakeholder groups

2. Stakeholder Analysis: Identifying direct users, indirect beneficiaries, potentially harmed groups, and broader society

3. Impact Evaluation and Prioritization: Assessing severity, likelihood, scope, and time horizons

4. Mitigation and Enhancement Measures: Strategies addressing negative impacts and amplifying positive ones

5. Documentation and Reporting: Transparent, structured records enabling internal decisions and external communication

LIFECYCLE APPROACH

Assessments occur at five critical points:
1. Design Phase: Preliminary assessments before development begins
2. Development Phase: Updates based on actual system performance and emerging behaviors
3. Pre-deployment: Comprehensive validation before operational launch
4. Operational Monitoring: Regular reassessment accounting for distributional shift and changing contexts
5. System Modifications: Reassessment whenever significant changes occur

KEY REQUIREMENTS AND PROCESSES

1. Structured Assessment Process: Organizations must establish consistent approaches tailored to their context, objectives, and risk appetite, considering both internal factors (governance, policies, contractual obligations) and external factors (legal requirements, cultural norms, competitive pressures).

2. Integration with Risk Management: Impact assessments should embed within existing risk, compliance, and management systems, aligning with privacy, human rights, and environmental impact assessments.

3. Timing and Triggers: Assessments occur at key lifecycle stages: design, deployment, and when significant changes arise. Reassessment is required if intended use, users, expectations, environment, or applicable laws change.

4. Scope and Responsibilities: Clear definition of which systems and stakeholders are covered, with allocated roles across the organization and documented ecosystem roles.

5. Thresholds for Sensitive/Restricted Uses: Organizations establish criteria identifying high-risk uses with escalation and approval processes based on legal requirements, stakeholder expectations, and ethical frameworks.

6. Documentation and Reporting: Comprehensive records of assessment processes, findings, and mitigation measures with clear reporting lines to management and external stakeholders where appropriate.

7. Approval, Monitoring and Review: Formal approval processes, ongoing monitoring, and periodic reviews ensure assessments remain current, with defined recording and reporting procedures.

8. Triaging and Assessment Necessity: Organizations use triaging tools to determine when full impact assessments are necessary, particularly for high-risk AI systems.

CHARACTERISTICS OF QUALITY ASSESSMENTS

A robust assessment includes:
- Detailed documentation of system description, functionalities, intended/unintended uses, data sources, algorithms, and deployment environment
- Systematic analysis of positive and negative impacts (accountability, transparency, fairness, privacy, reliability, safety, explainability, environmental effects)
- Stakeholder consultation including users, vulnerable groups, and internal teams
- Multidisciplinary input from legal, technical, ethical, and business experts
- Clear communication of outcomes, limitations, uncertainties, and mitigation measures
- Regular updates responding to system or environmental changes

INTEGRATION WITH EXISTING PROCESSES

Organizations should align AI impact assessments with:
- Privacy Impact Assessments
- Human Rights Impact Assessments
- Environmental Impact Assessments
- Security Impact Assessments
- Business and Financial Impact Assessments

REGULATORY ALIGNMENT

EU AI Act: High-risk systems must undergo conformity assessments; ISO/IEC 42005 assessments serve as evidence of due diligence.

Canada's AIDA: Proposes impact assessment requirements for high-risk AI; aligns with the standard's lifecycle and monitoring emphasis.

US Governance: Supports compliance with NIST AI Risk Management Framework and emerging executive orders.
