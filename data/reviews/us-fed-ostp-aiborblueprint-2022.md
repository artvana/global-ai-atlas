## Summary (verify for accuracy)

The OSTP Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights, released on October 4, 2022, is a non-binding framework articulating five principles for the design, development, and deployment of automated systems in the United States: (1) safe and effective systems, (2) algorithmic discrimination protections, (3) data privacy, (4) notice and explanation, and (5) human alternatives, consideration, and fallback. Published by the Biden White House Office of Science and Technology Policy (OSTP), the Blueprint is intended to guide both government and private sector actors and to inform future legislation and rulemaking. It does not carry the force of law and creates no enforceable rights.

## Key Obligations (verify completeness and accuracy)

- Systems should be tested, assessed, and monitored for safety and effectiveness before and after deployment (non-binding)
- Systems should be designed to prevent algorithmic discrimination based on protected characteristics
- Data collection should be limited to what is necessary; users should have meaningful consent over data use
- Users should be notified when automated systems make or inform decisions affecting them and receive plain-language explanations
- Users should be able to opt out of automated systems and access a human alternative where reasonably practicable

## Overview

The *Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights* is a non-binding framework published by the Biden administration's White House Office of Science and Technology Policy (OSTP) on October 4, 2022. It articulates five principles and associated practices that the U.S. government recommends for the design, development, deployment, and governance of automated systems that may meaningfully affect Americans' rights, opportunities, or access to critical resources and services.

The Blueprint does not carry the force of law and creates no enforceable individual rights. It is intended to guide both federal agencies and private sector actors, and to inform future legislation, regulation, and procurement. It was developed following a year-long process involving public listening sessions, technical workshops, and input from civil society, industry, and academic experts.
